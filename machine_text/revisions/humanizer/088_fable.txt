One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When our principal announced that every student has to join at least one extracurricular activity, kids started complaining before the announcement was even over. They said they have no time, that school should be done when the last bell rings, and that you can't force somebody to have fun. I get it, because I said some of that stuff too. But I thought about it more and I agree with the principal. One activity is a small thing to ask for. It only takes an hour or two a week, and I think students get a lot more out of it than that.

My biggest reason is that a lot of kids will never try anything unless somebody pushes them. In sixth grade I signed up for nothing. I went home, did my homework, and watched videos until dinner. Then in seventh grade my mom made me join the school newspaper because she was tired of seeing me on the couch. I was annoyed about it for around two weeks. Then I wrote a story about why the cafeteria ran out of pizza every Friday, and people I had never even talked to stopped me in the hall to talk about it. That was when I found out I like interviewing people. If it had been up to me I never would have known. The new rule does for every student what my mom did for me.

Activities also teach you things that classes don't. In math, if I don't do my work, the only thing that gets hurt is my own grade. Yearbook is different. If one person on the staff misses a deadline, forty pages go to the printer late and everybody pays for it. The student council treasurer has to explain to actual classmates why there isn't enough money for a dance and a field day both. And a goalkeeper who lets in three goals still has to show up to practice the next afternoon. In all of these, other people are depending on you and you are depending on them, which is something a worksheet can't teach.

Another reason is that activities help kids feel like they belong. Our school has several hundred students, and it can get lonely if the only people you know are the ones who sit near you. Clubs mix up kids who would never meet any other way. In chess club at our school, eighth graders teach sixth graders, and stage crew has kids from every lunch table. If you have even one group you belong to, then you have a reason to come to school on the days when classes aren't enough of a reason.

I know some people have good reasons for not liking the rule. Some students really are busy. They watch their little brothers and sisters, or they have jobs, or they ride a bus that leaves at 3:10. The principal should take that seriously and make the rule flexible. Some clubs could meet during lunch or advisory. Also, if a student already plays in a town soccer league or volunteers at a church, that should count. If the school does that, the rule isn't asking for much. It's one activity, the student picks it, and there are dozens to pick from.

Other people say that making it required takes the fun out of it. I think that's backward. Nobody is getting put on the debate team if they don't want to be. You still choose, and the list has robotics, art club, track, the literary magazine, and the garden committee on it. If you don't like anything on the list you can suggest something new. That is how our school got a cooking club.

School is supposed to get us ready for more than tests. It should help us figure out what we're good at and how to work with other people. If one activity is required, then every student gets that chance, including the ones who would never sign up by themselves. At the start of seventh grade I was mad that I got pushed into it. Now I'm glad somebody did.
