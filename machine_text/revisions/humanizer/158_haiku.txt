Summer projects are supposed to keep students learning when school is out, but who designs them matters as much as whether they exist. Summer projects should be designed by students within limits set by teachers, because students learn more deeply when they choose the question, and they are much more likely to finish a project they picked themselves.

Teacher-designed projects do have strengths. A teacher knows the curriculum, can connect the work to what students will face in September, and can grade fairly using a clear standard. That is why so many summer reading lists and worksheet packets exist. But these projects often fail for a simple reason: students are assigned them, not allowed to choose. A packet of fifty math problems arrives in June, gets copied during the last week of August, and teaches students that summer learning is something to get through.

Student-designed projects change that. Imagine a student who wants to know why bread rises. If she designs her own project, she might test three yeast temperatures, record the results in a notebook, and write a short report on fermentation. She practices measuring, analyzing data, and writing about science, the same skills a teacher might assign. The difference is that she practices them because she wants the answer. Another student who cares about his neighborhood might interview ten shop owners about how the street has changed and then make a simple map of what he learned. Neither project would appear on a standard list, but both require the planning, research, and persistence that schools hope to build.

Critics worry that students will pick easy projects and learn nothing. That worry is reasonable, but structure can solve it without taking control away. A teacher can approve each proposal, require a minimum standard for scope and evidence, and schedule a checkpoint in midsummer. The teacher sets the framework, and the student fills in the content. This kind of project asks more of students than a worksheet does, since they have to define a question, justify a method, and explain what their results mean.

Ownership also affects motivation. Research on self-determination suggests that people keep working longer on tasks they feel they chose. A student who proposes her own project cares about the outcome, and that care lasts into the school year. When she is asked to write a research question in history class, she already knows how to do it.

For these reasons, schools that want students to keep learning over the summer should let them choose what to study and then hold them to high standards for how they study it.
