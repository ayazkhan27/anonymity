Summer projects are a good idea. Anyone who has seen a classroom in September knows that two and a half months off can leave a student rusty at math, rusty at writing, and out of the habit of sitting down to think. But who designs the project matters as much as whether we assign one. I think summer projects should be designed by students, with the teacher helping at the start and at the end.

The biggest reason is motivation. A summer assignment has to compete with swimming pools, part-time jobs, family trips, and plenty of other stuff that feels more important right then. A packet of twenty worksheets from a teacher almost never wins that. It shows up as an obligation, and most students deal with obligations by doing the least they can, usually the last weekend of August. A project the student picked has a real chance, because they already want the answer. Say a girl loves baking. She could spend the summer testing how altitude, humidity, and oven temperature change how bread rises, and she would be doing actual chemistry without anyone telling her that. A guy who wants to build a skateboard ramp has to figure out angles, estimate what lumber costs, and write up a budget. The learning counts because the goal counts to them.

Student design also teaches planning, which a worksheet can't. In college and at a job, nobody gives you a list of problems with an answer key. You have to decide what to do, split it into steps, set deadlines, and notice when you are behind. Summer is a good time to practice since nothing big is on the line. If a student sets a goal that is too big and has to cut it down, they learn how to guess how long things take, and all it cost them was a few weeks of vacation. With a teacher-designed project, the student gets to hand all that thinking off to somebody else.

The obvious objection is that students will pick easy projects, or pick nothing. That is a fair worry, and it is why the teacher still has a job. Each student could turn in a short proposal in June that says what they plan to do, what they want to learn, and how they will show it. The teacher can approve it, ask for more depth, or point out a link to next year's class. If a student wants to "watch documentaries," the teacher can ask them to write a review comparing three. If a student wants to read a book, the teacher can ask for one harder than what they normally read. Then in the fall the student presents the finished work to the class. The proposal and the presentation let the teacher keep standards up while the student still owns the work.

Some people say teacher-designed projects make sure everyone practices the same basic skills. But a summer assignment is a bad way to do that anyway. Skills like fractions, grammar, and reading comprehension are better rebuilt in the first weeks of school, when a teacher can see who actually needs help. Summer should do what school can't, which is let a young person chase a question just because they are curious about it.

If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, the best project is one a student will finish and care about. Letting students design their own, while a teacher checks the plan and the result, keeps both the standards and the interest. It also shows that learning doesn't stop when the bell rings, since it belongs to the learner.
