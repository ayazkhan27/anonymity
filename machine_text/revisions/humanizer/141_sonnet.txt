Summer break is ten or twelve weeks long, and that is enough time for students to forget a lot of what they learned during the year. Teachers call this the summer slide, so a lot of schools give out summer work to keep kids thinking. What people don't agree on is who should plan that work. I think summer projects should be designed by the students, as long as the teacher gives them some rules to follow. If students get to pick the question they want to answer, they will remember more, and the project will actually be learning instead of just busywork.

The biggest reason is motivation. In the summer there is no bell and no teacher at the front of the room, so anything a student finishes is something they chose to finish. If a teacher hands out a packet of forty math problems, a fifteen-year-old has to give up beach days to do what feels exactly like a regular Tuesday worksheet. Most kids deal with this in the only way they can. They rush it the night before school starts or copy the answers from a friend. A project the student came up with is different. Say a boy loves basketball and decides to study how shooting angles change accuracy. He will measure and write down his shots and redo the math without anybody nagging him, because he wants to know the answer. Effort that comes from being curious lasts a lot longer than effort that comes from a deadline.

Student projects also teach things a teacher's project can't. Picking a topic, deciding what counts as proof, making a schedule, and finishing without anyone watching are things adults have to do on their own. When a teacher designs the project, the hardest part is already done, because the student just follows directions. If a student has to plan it herself, she goes through the whole process of learning something. For example, a girl who is annoyed by trash in her neighborhood park could count and sort the litter for six weeks and then write a letter to the city asking for more recycling bins. That uses science, math, writing, and some understanding of how local government works, and no worksheet could really lay that out for her. Since she built the project herself, she knows why every step is there.

Some people worry that students will pick something easy or do nothing at all. A kid could say he is going to "watch documentaries" and call it research. I think that is a fair worry, but it is a reason for the teacher to give guidance, not a reason to take the choice away from students. The best way is for the teacher and student to work together. Before school ends, the teacher says what every project needs: a question, a method, a record of the work, and a final product to share in the fall. Then each student turns in a short proposal, and the teacher either approves it or sends it back to be fixed. That way the work stays challenging but the student still picks the subject. Teachers can tell a vague idea from one that will work, and a short talk in June can turn "watch documentaries" into "compare how three documentaries show the same historical event and decide which one is most accurate."

Another problem people bring up is fairness. Some students have books, computers, and parents who can help at home, and others don't. If the teacher assigns the project, at least everyone gets the same task. But the same task does not give the same results, because the student with more resources will still finish faster and do it better. Student design might actually help close the gap, since a kid can build a project out of whatever she has. A student with no internet could interview her grandmother about the family's history, write a short biography, and draw a map of the places her family has lived. All that takes is a notebook and a conversation. Teachers can help even more by giving out a list of free ideas and by letting students present in different ways, like an essay, a model, a video, or a speech.

Student-designed projects would also make the first week of September better. A teacher who collects packets gets a stack of work that all looks alike. With student projects the teacher gets a whole room of different things people found out. Students have something real to share, and they learn from their classmates. Listening to someone explain what she found out about the litter in the park is much more interesting than reading a pile of answers to the same twenty questions.

Summer work should keep students learning, but it should also let them enjoy it. Teachers know what a good project needs, and students know what they care about. If teachers set the standards and students pick the topics, schools can get hard work and real interest at the same time. That is what could turn a required assignment into something students might do even if nobody asked them to.
