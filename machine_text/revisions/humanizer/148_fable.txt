Every June my cousin came home with the same packet. It was forty pages of reading passages and math review, stapled at the corner, and it was due on the first day of school. Every August he filled it out at the kitchen table in the last four days before the deadline, and he complained the whole time. He did finish it every year, but I doubt he could have told you one thing that was in it by October. That packet is the main reason I think students should design their own summer projects, as long as teachers set some limits on them.

My biggest reason is that summer work only gets done if the student decides to do it. During the school year a teacher can check in every day and notice when somebody is falling behind. In July nobody is watching. I think a project that a student picked has a much better chance of lasting ten weeks with no supervision than one a teacher handed out. For example, a girl who loves cooking could spend the summer testing how the amount of baking soda changes a batch of cookies. She would record her results and write up what she found. That is practice with measurement, ratios and the scientific method, and she would be doing it because she wanted to know the answer. If you gave that same girl a worksheet on ratios, she would do it the night before it was due, just like my cousin.

Designing a project also teaches students how to plan, which we almost never have to do. Usually the assignment sheet already tells us the topic, what the final product should look like, and how to break the work into steps. Colleges and employers expect people to handle open-ended tasks, and a summer project is a low-risk place to try one. Say a student with no coding experience decides to build a working app. A few weeks in, he realizes it is too big and scales it back to a simple quiz game. He lost a few frustrating weeks, but he learned how much he can really take on, and I don't think a packet could teach him that.

There is also a fairness problem that I think people overlook. Students spend their summers very differently. Some travel, some work thirty hours a week, some watch their younger siblings all day, and some don't have reliable internet. When a teacher designs one project for everybody, it assumes everybody has the same kind of summer. A required museum visit or an online research assignment might not be possible for a student who works at her family's restaurant. But she could design a project where she tracks the restaurant's busiest hours and proposes a better staffing schedule, and that would fit into the life she already has.

The usual objection is that students will pick something easy, or that they won't cover what the school needs them to learn. I think this is a fair worry, and it is why teachers should still be part of the process. A teacher can require a written proposal in May and hand out a rubric ahead of time. The teacher can also list the skills the project has to show, like research from at least three sources or a certain amount of writing. If somebody proposes to "watch movies all summer," the teacher sends it back. If somebody proposes to compare three film adaptations to the novels they came from, the teacher approves it. So the standards still come from the teacher, even though the idea comes from the student.

Teachers know what students need to learn, but students know what they will really do in the middle of July. A summer project needs both of those things to work. That is why I believe students should design their summer projects and teachers should set the rules they have to follow.
