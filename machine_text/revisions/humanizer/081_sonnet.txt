I agree with the principal's decision that every student should have to join at least one extracurricular activity. Some kids will complain at first, and a few will say their afternoons are already full. But school is supposed to get us ready for more than tests, and a required activity gives everybody a chance to learn stuff a classroom doesn't really teach.

The best reason is that these activities build skills that grades don't show. Take the yearbook. To finish one book, a team of students has to plan the pages, take and sort photos, write captions, and get everything to the printer on time. If one person falls behind, the whole book is late. A student who spends a year on that team learns to manage time, take feedback from an editor, and count on classmates while they count on you. Student council is like that too. A council member who helps put together a fall dance has to make a budget, get teachers to help, and deal with complaints when the music is too loud. Those are the same abilities employers say they want, and most students don't get much practice with them in a normal class schedule.

Another reason is that extracurriculars help students find where they fit in. Every school has quiet students who walk in, sit through their classes, and leave without really knowing anyone. I have seen this happen. A boy in my grade barely talked until he joined the cross-country team. He was not the fastest runner, but people welcomed him on the long bus rides and at team dinners, and by spring he was cheering loudly for teammates he had never met the fall before. When you belong to something, you want to come to school. Research on attendance and dropout rates often finds that students who are connected to a club or team are less likely to quit school early. If the rule gets even a few lonely students to find a group, it was worth it.

Some people say a requirement takes away choice. They think students should only join what they really care about, and that forced members will be bored and careless. That is a fair worry, but the decision leaves lots of room to choose. The principal did not say everybody has to play football. The list has sports, yearbook, and student council, and a school could add chess club, drama, band, a recycling committee, or a robotics team. A student who doesn't like competition can paint sets for the spring play. A student who loves computers can run the school website. The rule only says everyone has to pick something. Picking from many options is still a real choice, and it pushes students to try something they might never have thought of. A lot of us don't know what we enjoy until we try it.

Time is another worry. Some students have jobs, take care of younger brothers or sisters, or have a lot of homework. A good policy should think about that. The school can have activities that meet at lunch or just once a week, and it can let a part-time job or family duties count in special cases. Being flexible keeps the rule fair without ruining the point of it. Even a busy student gets something out of one small commitment, because it gives them a routine with other people in it and something to look forward to.

Last, the habit matters after high school. Adults who volunteer, coach a youth team, or sit on a neighborhood board usually started by joining things when they were young. If you learn early that you can help a group and the group is better because of you, you are more ready to be an active citizen. A school that asks all its students to practice that is investing in the community they will lead someday.

For these reasons, I support the principal's decision. Extracurricular activities teach teamwork and responsibility, they help lonely students connect, and they give everyone a chance to find a talent. With good options and some flexibility, asking each student to do one activity is a small thing to ask for a big reward.
