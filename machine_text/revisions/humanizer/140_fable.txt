Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June a lot of students go home with a packet stuffed in with their report card. That packet is the summer project, and it is supposed to keep our brains working during the ten weeks we are out of school. I get why schools do this. Research on the "summer slide" shows that students can lose a month or more of reading and math progress between June and September. What I want to argue about is who gets to decide what the project is. I think students should design their own summer projects, and teachers should set a few clear requirements and approve each plan before school lets out.

My biggest reason is motivation. During the school year a teacher can remind you, encourage you, and check in on how things are going. In July there is nobody looking over your shoulder. The only thing that gets a project done well, and not thrown together the night before school starts, is that the student cares about it. A project the teacher designs is the same for everybody, which means it doesn't really fit anybody. One summer I was assigned a packet of thirty reading response worksheets. I did all of them in two days at the end of August by copying the format from one page to the next, and a week later I couldn't have told you what any of the books were about. A friend of mine had a science class where she was allowed to pick her own topic. She spent the summer testing which homemade fertilizer grew the tallest tomato plants in her backyard. She measured them every three days and came back in September with a notebook full of data and a real opinion about compost. Nobody had to nag her because it was her project.

Letting students design the project also makes sense because we don't all have the same summer. One student works thirty hours a week at a grocery store. Another one lives with grandparents in a different state, and somebody else has to watch younger siblings every day. A teacher writing one assignment in May can't plan for all of that, but a student can build the project around the summer they are going to have. The student with the grocery job could track how prices change over the summer and explain why. The one staying with grandparents could record interviews about what the town was like fifty years ago and write a family history. Both of those take reading, writing, math, and research, and a teacher would never have thought to assign them because the teacher had no way of knowing about the job or the trip.

Designing a project is also a skill that is worth learning. You have to choose a question, decide how you are going to answer it, guess how long each step will take, and change the plan when something goes wrong. Colleges and employers say these are the abilities young people are missing. When the teacher designs the project, the teacher does all of that thinking and the student is left with only the labor. If the student has to plan the work, then the planning is part of what they learn.

Some people would say that students will pick something easy if you leave it up to them, and some would. I can picture a student proposing to watch a TV series and write one paragraph about it. People also point out that teachers know what skills the next grade needs and students don't. I think both of these are fair points. To me, though, they are reasons to give students guidance, and they don't justify taking the design away from students completely. The teacher can require that every project have a certain amount of reading, a written product of a set length, and some connection to next year's subjects. Students would turn in a one page proposal in May, and the teacher would send the weak ones back to be revised. The teacher protects the quality that way, and the project still belongs to the student.

Schools give summer projects because they want learning to keep going while the building is closed. If no one is supervising, the learning has to come from the student. I think a project that a student chose, planned, and fit around their own summer is much more likely to be finished with care and remembered afterward than a packet handed to the whole class. So schools should set the standards and then let students design the work.
