Phones and the Driver's Seat

Pretty much everyone I know has a phone, and pretty much everyone I know who drives has looked at it behind the wheel. I've done it too. It was at a red light, and I told myself it didn't count because the car wasn't moving. When people ask if drivers should be allowed to use cell phones in any capacity, they usually want a flat yes or a flat no. I don't think either answer is right. Drivers should not be allowed to hold a phone, read it, or type on it while the car is moving or stopped in traffic. A phone that is sitting in a mount giving directions, or one that is being used for an emergency call, is different and should stay legal.

The reason to ban handheld use is pretty simple. To drive safely you need your eyes on the road, your hands on the wheel, and your mind on what is happening in front of you. Texting takes away all three at the same time. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has said that reading a text takes a driver's eyes off the road for about five seconds. At highway speed that is about the length of a football field that you cover without looking. Nobody would agree to drive that far with their eyes closed, but that is basically what a quick look at a message is. The same agency says distracted driving causes thousands of deaths in the United States every year. The real number is probably higher, because a driver who caused a crash doesn't have much reason to admit a phone was involved.

Phones are also worse than other distractions because the person who gets hurt is often somebody else. A driver who is eating a sandwich or messing with the radio is distracted too, but a phone pulls on your attention harder, since a call or a message wants an answer back. A person in the crosswalk or on a bike on the shoulder never agreed to that risk, and neither did the family in the next lane. This is the kind of situation laws are for, when one person's convenience costs another person their safety. We have speed limits and drunk driving laws for the same reason, and hardly anyone argues now that those are unfair limits on freedom.

Some people say hands-free calling solves the problem. It solves part of it. If you are on speaker or a headset, you have both hands on the wheel and both eyes forward, which is clearly better than holding the phone. But studies of driver attention have found that part of your mind stays in the conversation, and your reaction time gets slower even when your hands are free. A passenger will stop talking when traffic gets bad because the passenger can see the road. The person on the other end of a call can't see it. I still would not ban hands-free calls completely, since a law like that would be almost impossible to enforce. I also would not act like they are harmless. Drivers should keep those calls short. New drivers, who are still learning how to handle the car, should not be allowed to make them at all.

Some uses of a phone make driving safer, and a total ban would get rid of those. Navigation is the obvious one. A phone in a dashboard mount that reads the directions out loud is a lot less dangerous than a lost driver slowing down to read street signs or unfolding a paper map. Another one is being able to call for help after a crash, or to report a drunk driver or a hazard on the road. A law that bans phones "in any capacity" would make criminals out of people doing these things. When a law is so strict that sensible people ignore it, they start to respect the sensible parts of it less too.

So the rule I would defend is easy to remember. Set your route before you pull out, put the phone in the mount, and don't touch it again until the car is parked. If something really can't wait, pull over. The message will still be there in ten minutes, and nobody gets hurt waiting for it.
