Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June my cousin comes home with a packet in his backpack. It has a reading list of three novels, a worksheet for each one, and a rubric stapled to the back. Then every August, the night before school starts, he sits at the kitchen table and fills in the worksheets using chapter summaries he found online. He gets full credit for it. I don't think he has learned anything from those packets, except maybe how fast he can write when he is panicking. His school wants kids to keep learning over the summer, which I agree with, but the packet is a bad way to do it. I think summer projects should be designed by the students, with teachers setting the limits and checking the plan.

My first reason is that people work harder on things they picked themselves. During the school year a teacher can push a student through an assignment he doesn't care about, because the teacher is right there five days a week. In the summer nobody is there. So the only thing that will keep a project going through July is if the student is interested in it. Say a girl loves baking and decides to test how different amounts of baking soda change a cookie. She is probably going to run that experiment six or seven times, partly because she gets to eat the results and partly because she really does want to know which batch comes out flat. She will end up measuring and writing down what happened and comparing the batches, which is basically the scientific method, and nobody forced her to do it. If you gave that same girl a packet on the phases of the moon, she would do exactly what my cousin does.

My second reason is that designing a project is a skill too. When a teacher hands you a finished assignment, the hardest thinking is already done. Somebody else chose the question, decided what counts as an answer, and figured out how long each part should take. Students almost never get to practice those steps, and then we get to college or a job and we are expected to already know how. If a student plans to interview her grandfather about leaving Vietnam, she has to decide what to ask and how to record it. She also has to figure out what to do when he doesn't want to talk about a certain year. A student who wants to build a birdhouse has to price the wood, and then he finds out he measured wrong. Problems like that are frustrating, but I think they teach more than a rubric does.

Another reason is that summers are not equal. A project designed by a teacher assumes everybody has the same summer, and that isn't true. One student might be at a lake for two months while another one is watching her little brothers every day because her parents work, and somebody else has a job at a grocery store. A project that requires visiting a museum or having fast internet is easy for some of those kids and nearly impossible for others. If students design their own, they can build it around the summer they actually have. The girl who is babysitting could teach her four-year-old brother to read and study how he learns. The boy at the grocery store could track prices for ten weeks and graph them.

The best argument against my idea is that some students will pick something too easy, or they won't pick anything. I think that is a real problem, and it is the reason teachers should still be part of the process. Before school lets out, each student should turn in a one-page proposal with the question, the plan, and what the final product will be. The teacher approves it or sends it back with comments. The teacher can also require every project to include some reading, some writing, and some kind of evidence. That way the standards still belong to the teacher and the subject belongs to the student. Also, a teacher who reads thirty proposals in June gets to read thirty different projects in September instead of thirty copies of the same worksheet, which has to be more interesting for her too.

The whole point of a summer project is to keep students learning when no one is making them. My cousin's packet has never done that for him. I think a question he actually wanted answered would have a much better chance.
