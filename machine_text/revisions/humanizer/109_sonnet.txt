Drivers Should Not Use Cell Phones While Driving

Almost everybody has a cell phone these days, and most of us pick it up dozens of times a day without even thinking about it. That is fine on the couch or at a desk. In a car it is dangerous. I think drivers should not be allowed to use handheld cell phones at all while they are driving, and the law should treat the phone like the hazard it is.

The biggest problem is attention. Driving seems automatic, but you are constantly making judgments. You watch the car ahead of you, check your mirrors, notice a pedestrian by the curb, and guess what the other drivers are going to do. A phone uses the same parts of your brain. Researchers talk about three kinds of distraction: visual, when your eyes leave the road, manual, when your hands leave the wheel, and cognitive, when your mind leaves the job. Texting does all three at once. If a driver looks at a message for five seconds on the highway, the car travels about the length of a football field with nobody watching the road. No feature in a modern car can make up for that.

The numbers support this too. Distracted driving kills thousands of people in the United States every year, and the real number is probably higher because drivers almost never admit they were on their phones after a crash. A lot of the victims were not even the distracted driver. They were passengers, cyclists, kids walking to school, and other drivers who did everything right. That is why I do not think this is just about personal freedom. Risking your own life is your choice. Risking the lives of strangers is not.

Some people say hands-free calling is safe enough and a ban should only cover texting. It sounds reasonable, but the evidence does not back it up. Studies with driving simulators and real traffic data found that the conversation itself is distracting, not only holding the phone. Drivers in the middle of a call miss more traffic signals and react slower when the car ahead brakes suddenly, even with both hands on the wheel and both eyes forward. The brain cannot give itself fully to a conversation and to the road at the same moment. It switches back and forth, and every switch costs time that a driver may not have. Hands-free fixes the problem with the hands and leaves the problem with the mind.

Other people say drivers already talk to passengers or change the radio, so phones are no different. A passenger is in the same car, though, and usually goes quiet when traffic gets tense. Someone on the phone cannot see the intersection and keeps talking. Changing a radio station is also quick and simple, while a phone has endless messages, notifications, and apps that are made to hold your attention. A phone is designed to be hard to ignore, which is the wrong quality for something inside a moving car.

Enforcement is a fair worry, because a law does not work if nobody follows it. But seat belt laws and drunk driving laws show that firm rules change behavior over time. When the penalties are real and people understand why, something that used to seem normal starts to feel irresponsible. A clear rule is also easier to enforce than a bunch of exceptions. If an officer sees a phone in a driver's hand, there is no need to guess whether the driver was dialing, reading, or doing something that was allowed.

There should be a few exceptions. A driver in a real emergency, like reporting a crash or calling for help, should be able to use the phone, and setting up a navigation screen before the trip starts is reasonable. Pulling over and parking before using the phone is always possible. These exceptions are small and they do not change the main point that a car trip is not the time for calls, texts, or scrolling.

Nobody has to be reachable every minute of the day. Getting somewhere a little later or letting a message wait costs almost nothing. The other choice can cost a life. Drivers should put the phone away until the car is parked, and the law should require it.
