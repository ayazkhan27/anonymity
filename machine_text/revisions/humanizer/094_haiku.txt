Every student should have to join at least one extracurricular activity. Some students will hate this rule. They think school is for classes, and they say a required activity wastes time that could go to homework, a part-time job, or sleep. I understand that concern. But I think the principal made the right decision. Required activities give students a place to belong, they teach skills that classrooms often miss, and they can show talents that would otherwise stay hidden.

The biggest benefit is belonging. Many students start high school feeling invisible. They sit in big classes, eat lunch fast, and go home without talking to anyone outside their small group of friends. An activity can change that. Take a student who joins yearbook staff because it was the only elective left in his schedule. At first he only takes pictures of the cafeteria and the football field. Over the year he learns to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and argue with editors about page layouts. By spring he knows dozens of people from every grade, and the teachers who advised the staff have become his mentors. Without the requirement, he might never have walked into that darkroom.

Activities also teach things that tests do not measure. A student on the debate team learns to research both sides of an issue and speak calmly when everyone is watching. A soccer player learns that one missed pass affects the whole team, and that practice on a cold Tuesday matters as much as the game on Friday. Student council members learn how to budget when they have to spend two thousand dollars from a fundraiser. They also learn to compromise when three classes want three different homecoming themes. Students learn these lessons by doing them, and the requirement makes sure everyone gets that chance.

Some people say forcing students to join turns activities into chores. That can be true for a student who gets placed in a club he hates. But the rule does not require a specific choice. It only requires that students try something. A student who dislikes sports can pick chess, robotics, or the literary magazine. A student who is scared of public speaking can join photography or the school garden. Most people find out what they like only after trying a few things. The requirement makes sure that trying happens. Many adults can name one hobby that changed their lives, and a lot of those hobbies started because they had to try something.

Finally, activities connect what students learn in class to the real world. A teacher can explain how a government works, but student council shows how a vote is counted and how an idea becomes a rule. A teacher can describe teamwork, but a band or robotics team needs teamwork every afternoon. These experiences help students get ready for jobs, college, and their community. In all of those places, working with others and taking initiative count for a lot, not just grades.

Someone will argue that one rule cannot fit every student. That is true, and it is the reason the rule should be broad. Students should have many choices so each one can find a good fit. The principal understood that school should do more than hand out information. It should help students become confident, connected, and curious. For those reasons, I agree that every student should join at least one extracurricular activity.
