Every June students leave school with the same packet. There's a reading log, some math worksheets, and maybe a book report with the rubric stapled on the front. By August most kids have either rushed through it in one panicked weekend or just never done it. If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, I think schools should let students design their own projects and have teachers help guide them instead of making the whole thing.

My biggest reason is motivation. When a teacher designs the project, every student has to care about the same thing at the same time, and that almost never happens. Say a kid loves baseball. He'd probably groan if he got a regular essay assignment, but he might spend hours figuring out batting averages, tracking pitch speeds, and comparing players from different decades. That's actual statistics and he'd be doing it because he wants to. When students get to pick the topic, the project stops feeling like a chore that followed them home and starts feeling like it's theirs. I also think stuff you learn because you're curious sticks a lot longer than stuff you learn just to check a box.

Another reason is that planning a project is a skill people need to practice. In school almost everything is decided for us, like what to read, when it's due, and how long it has to be. Adults have to set their own goals all the time and figure out how they're going to reach them. A summer project is a pretty low stakes way to practice that. For example, a girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden would have to look up what plants grow in her climate, budget for seeds and soil, stick to a watering schedule, and figure out what went wrong when her tomatoes split open. A worksheet can't teach you that kind of planning.

Also, when students design their own projects they can use what they actually have over the summer. Every family's summer is different. One kid spends July at his grandparents' farm, another one works part time at a restaurant, and another one babysits her little brothers and sisters every day. One assignment from a teacher can't fit all of those lives, but a project the student designs can. The kid at the restaurant could study how the kitchen keeps track of food costs, and the babysitter could write and illustrate a picture book for the kids she watches.

Some people will say that if teachers don't design the projects, students will pick something way too easy or just give up on it. That's a fair point, but it means there should be some structure, not that teachers have to make every decision. Schools could have students turn in a short proposal before summer starts that explains what they're going to do, what they want to learn, and how they'll show it. Teachers could approve it or suggest changes and then check in once or twice over email. At the end students could present to the class, so they're held accountable and everyone gets to learn from each other's summers.

Summer is one of the only times students get to follow their own interests. Schools should use that freedom instead of filling it up with another packet. If students design their own projects, they'll learn the material and they'll also learn how to teach themselves.
