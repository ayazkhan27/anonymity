When people ask for advice, they usually get one answer, and that answer can start to feel like the right one. A friend says to take the job. A parent says to stay home. After hearing just one of them, a decision can seem finished before anyone has really looked at it. Asking more than one person helps because it gives you something to compare. Most good choices come out of comparing options.

Every person who gives advice sees the situation from their own point of view. Their experience, their values, and their past mistakes all affect what they notice. A former teacher might think about whether a graduate program will look good on a resume. A current grad student might think about how many hours the program takes and whether the pay covers rent. Neither one is wrong, but each one leaves something out. Hearing both helps a person ask a better question: what does the program cost in time and money, and what does it give back in career opportunities?

Multiple opinions also show where people disagree, and those disagreements can be the most useful part. Say someone is thinking about buying a used car. One mechanic says the engine sounds good. Another notices a worn timing belt that could cost a thousand dollars to replace. That disagreement shows exactly where to look more closely. Without the second opinion, the buyer might have believed the car was in fine shape.

Getting more than one opinion also protects against bias. Advice from one person can carry that person's bias, and nobody may notice. A salesperson who recommends an expensive laptop obviously wants to make a sale. A friend who always values stability might discourage any kind of risk. When several people with different reasons for their advice speak up, these biases tend to balance out. The person deciding can then see which recommendations are based on facts and which are based on personal feelings.

Still, more opinions are not always better. A person can collect so many views that they cannot decide anything. Or they can just pick the answer they already wanted to hear. The goal is not to count votes. The goal is to understand the reasons behind each opinion and compare them with what matters to you. A student choosing a college might hear from a parent who cares about prestige, a counselor who cares about cost, and a recent graduate who wishes they had picked a school closer to home. The useful step is deciding which of these concerns matters most for that student, instead of letting the loudest voice win.

Good advice-seeking also means picking advisors who know different things. Asking three people from the same background may get three similar answers and not much new information. It works better to ask one person with direct experience, one person with professional knowledge, and one person who knows the decision-maker well. Together, they can give a fuller picture.

In the end, the person who has to live with the decision still has to make it. Multiple opinions do not take that responsibility away. What they do give is a wider view of the options, a clearer idea of the trade-offs, and a better chance of noticing details that would otherwise be missed. That is why asking more than one person so often leads to a better choice.
