Every June students leave school with a summer packet, and every August a lot of them come back with it finished in one rushed weekend. I know because I've done it. Summer projects are supposed to keep our brains working over break, but whether they actually do depends on how they're set up. I think students should design their own summer projects, with teachers giving guidelines and checkpoints along the way. When students pick what they study they work harder and learn more, and they also practice skills a worksheet can't teach.

The first reason is motivation. Summer is the only time of year when students have real freedom, so a project that ignores what they're interested in feels like a punishment for having a vacation. Take a teacher-designed assignment like reading three assigned novels and answering questions on each one. Most people do it the last week because nobody cares about it. Now picture a student who loves baseball and decides to track batting averages all summer, make graphs, and figure out which players got better. He's doing statistics and nobody is making him, and he'll probably keep going after he hits the minimum. If students aren't interested, summer learning mostly doesn't happen.

My second reason is that designing a project is a skill by itself. In school the teacher usually gives us the question, the materials and the due date. Adults don't get instructions that clear very often. If a student has to choose a topic, plan out the steps, find resources and manage their time over ten weeks, they're practicing the kind of independence college and jobs expect. One summer my cousin decided she wanted to learn how to fix old bicycles. She watched tutorials, bought parts on a budget, and kept notes on what didn't work. By September she had fixed four bikes and sold two of them, and she learned more about planning than any packet would have taught her.

Third, students are different from each other, and student-designed projects take that into account. One project for the whole class assumes everybody has the same interests, the same resources and the same summer schedule. That isn't true. One kid might spend July babysitting younger siblings while another one travels or works a part-time job. If students design their own projects, each of them can find something that actually fits their life, like writing a family history, starting a garden, or coding a simple game.

Some people argue that if students are left on their own they'll pick easy projects or just do nothing. That's a fair worry, but I think it means projects need structure, and it doesn't mean students shouldn't get a choice. Teachers could have students turn in a short proposal before school ends, set some standards for effort and evidence, and ask for a quick presentation in the fall. Students would still own their projects and teachers could still hold them accountable.

The point of summer projects is to keep students learning, and keeping them busy isn't the same thing. If students design their own work with some help from a teacher, they'll care about it, get better at thinking for themselves, and come back in the fall with something they're proud to show people.
