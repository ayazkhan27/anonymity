Summer projects should be designed by students. Teachers already control most of the school year, and a summer assignment handed down from above often turns into a chore to finish instead of something worth learning. When students pick their own projects, they actually care about them.

Take a student who loves baseball but dreads her required reading. If she designs a project comparing batting statistics across decades, she has to learn statistics, research baseball history, and write clearly about what she finds. Assigned a generic book report instead, she would probably skim the book and forget it by September.

Student design does not mean no guidance. Teachers can set rules, like a minimum number of hours, a final product, and a short proposal that needs approval. That structure keeps projects serious while letting students make their own choices.

Ownership also builds the skill that matters most after high school: figuring out what to learn and how to learn it. A project a teacher designs teaches compliance; one a student designs teaches independence.
