Every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some people say a school cannot force a student to spend time outside of class on something he or she did not choose. I understand that concern, but I agree with the principal's decision. Required participation does not mean everyone will have fun. It means every student gets a chance to find a place where he or she belongs, and for many students that place is not in a math textbook or a lecture hall.

The most obvious benefit is that activities give students a reason to show up. Many students who struggle in class have a hard time seeing why school matters. When a student joins the track team, coming to school makes more sense. He has practice after lunch, teammates who expect him there, and a coach who notices when he misses. I once watched a student who barely passed freshman English become the most reliable member of the yearbook staff. She had to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and fix layouts late at night. Her grades got better because she finally had a reason to care about what she did during the day.

Extracurricular activities also teach skills that classrooms rarely cover. Student council, for example, asks students to plan events, raise money, and argue for their ideas in front of adults who may disagree with them. A student who runs a food drive learns how to budget, how to ask for donations politely, and how to recover when a plan falls apart. These lessons will not show up on a standardized test, but employers and colleges do care about them. Sports teach discipline and how to lose well. Debate teaches a student to listen to the other side before answering. Music ensembles show that one person's mistake affects everyone in the room.

Some critics argue that students who already have jobs, family responsibilities, or heavy class loads cannot fit in another commitment. That concern deserves respect, and a good policy should account for it. However, the requirement does not need to be strict. A student can choose from many options, including clubs that meet once a week or service projects that take a few hours a month. The school can also count volunteer work at a library or community center. The goal is not to fill every afternoon with practice. The goal is to make sure every student has at least one connection to the school beyond the bell schedule.

Opponents also say that a student who is not interested in sports or drama will be miserable in a required activity. But the variety of options makes it unlikely that anyone will find nothing of interest. A student who dislikes competition might enjoy photography, robotics, or chess club. A shy student might find that the gardening club gives him a quiet place to talk with others. Many students discover interests they never knew they had because someone asked them to try something new. Ask an adult with a lifelong hobby how it started, and many will say they tried it by accident.

Finally, schools have a responsibility to prepare students for life after graduation, and adult life usually involves working with other people. People work in teams, lead projects, and deal with conflict. Extracurricular activities are a low-pressure place to practice those skills. A student who has spent a season on a team or a year on a committee walks into college or a first job with experience that a transcript alone cannot show.

For these reasons, I believe the principal is right. Requiring at least one activity is not an attempt to control students' free time. It is a chance to discover something that might change how a student sees school and himself. The policy will not work perfectly for everyone, but it gives every student a door to walk through, and that is worth the small effort it asks of each of them.
