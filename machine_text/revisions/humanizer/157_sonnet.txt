Summer Projects Should Be Student-Designed

Every June, students leave school with a long stretch of free time, and in a lot of districts they also get a packet of assignments so their brains don't go soft. I get the goal. Studies on the "summer slide" say students can lose a good part of their math and reading skills over the break. But who designs the work matters a lot. Summer projects should be student-designed, with teachers helping out as guides, because when students own the project they put in more effort, they learn for real, and they build skills a worksheet can't.

The biggest reason is motivation. A teacher-designed project shows up in the middle of summer as one more thing you have to do, and students treat it that way. They put it off until August, copy answers from a friend, or rush it the night before school starts. None of it belongs to them. When students pick their own topics, that changes. Say a student loves basketball. He could spend the summer looking at shooting percentages and building a statistical model that predicts which players will improve the most. Give that same student a generic packet of probability problems and he will probably do the bare minimum. Being interested in something doesn't run out, and student-designed projects use that.

Another reason is that designing a project teaches you something too. When a teacher assigns the work, the hardest part of real work gets hidden, which is deciding what to do. Someone already picked the question, the format, and the deadline. A student who has to write a proposal has to take a vague interest and turn it into a question she can actually answer, guess how long each stage will take, and plan for the problems that will come up. College professors and employers say young people are missing exactly these habits. Think about a girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden and keep track of how it does. She has to budget, look up soil conditions, write down observations, and change her plan when the tomatoes die. She learns science, but she also learns to manage her own time, and a packet never teaches that.

Some people will say that students left on their own will pick easy projects or do nothing. That is a fair worry, but it is a reason to add structure, not a reason to let teachers control everything. Schools can require a short proposal in June that the teacher reads and approves, plus a few check-ins during the summer and a presentation in the fall. The teacher sets the standard, like requiring research, a written reflection, and some proof the student kept working, and the student picks the subject. A teacher can also push a weak idea to something better. If a student wants to "watch documentaries," the teacher might ask him to compare how three films show the same historical event and write about the differences. The teacher's knowledge makes the work better without taking it away from the student.

Another objection is fairness. If every student gets the same assignment, grading is easy and everybody is treated equally. But treating everyone the same is not the same as being fair. Students come back from summer with very different resources. One might have a library card and a quiet house, while another has a part-time job and watches younger siblings. A strict assignment that expects museum trips, travel, or a computer favors the kids who already have more. With a flexible student-designed project, each person can make something that fits their life. The student with the job could interview coworkers about what they do and write an essay about the local economy, and all that takes is a notebook and some curiosity.

Last, think about what students actually remember. Hardly anyone remembers a worksheet from the summer after sixth grade. A lot of people remember the time they built a birdhouse, wrote a short story, or taught themselves to code a simple game. Those things stick because the person made them. A student who finishes a project they chose goes back to school with something to show and the confidence that comes from making it themselves.

Learning over the summer matters, and schools are right to want to protect it. The best way to do that is to trust students to design the work, hold them accountable with proposals and presentations, and let teachers coach instead of give orders. When the learning belongs to the student, it lasts long after September.
