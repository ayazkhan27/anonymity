Summer Projects Should Belong to Students

Every June a lot of schools send students home with a summer project so our brains don't turn to mush over the break. I get why. Ten weeks with no reading, writing, or math can make you forget a lot of what you learned that year. But the real question is who should design these projects. I think students should design their own summer projects, and teachers should just set some basic expectations and help out when needed. When students pick their own projects they actually try harder, they learn skills that school doesn't really teach, and learning feels like something they own instead of something being done to them.

The first reason is motivation. A project the teacher designs, like a report on an assigned novel, usually gets done the night before school starts, rushed and hated. Summer is the only time of year students really control their own time, and an assignment that feels like more homework can't compete with everything else going on. A project you picked yourself is different. Say a girl loves baking. She could study the chemistry of why bread rises by trying different amounts of yeast and writing down what happens. A guy who is obsessed with basketball could track his free throw percentage all summer and learn how to graph it and figure out what the numbers mean. Since these projects come from things students already like, they keep coming back to them without being forced, and that is exactly what keeps learning going over the break.

Second, designing a project is a lesson by itself. During the school year teachers decide pretty much everything: what we read, what questions we answer, when stuff is due. We almost never get to practice planning a long assignment, setting goals, or changing plans when something doesn't work. When a student has to come up with a question, figure out how to answer it, and keep the work going for several weeks, they are practicing the kind of independence that college and jobs expect. If someone's garden experiment dies in July, they have to figure out what went wrong and start over. No worksheet teaches you to keep going like that.

Third, letting students design projects respects the fact that everyone is different. One assignment for the whole class assumes every student needs the same practice and likes the same things. That's not true. One student might need to work on her writing while another one is ready for an advanced coding project. If students get to choose, each person can work at the top of their own level instead of the average of the class.

Some people say that if students are left alone they will pick projects that are way too easy or just not do anything. That's a fair point, but it means students need guidance, not total teacher control. Teachers could have students turn in a short proposal before summer, set rules for how much work is expected, and have everyone present their project in the fall. With that kind of structure, freedom and responsibility can work together.

The point of summer projects is to keep students curious, and nothing does that better than letting them work on what they already care about.
