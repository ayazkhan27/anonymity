I agree with the principal's decision. Making every student join at least one extracurricular activity might sound like one more thing for busy people to do, but I think it would make our school better and help students more than most of them expect.

My first reason is that a lot of students never try anything new unless somebody pushes them. When I started high school, I told myself I was "not a club person." I went to class, went home, and did my homework. Then a teacher asked me to help the yearbook staff take photos at a pep rally, just one time. I ended up staying the whole year. It was not even my idea, but it became the best part of my year. I learned how to crop a photo, meet a deadline, and argue politely with an editor about which picture should get a full page. If nobody had pushed me, I would never have found out that I liked it. A requirement works the same way. It gives shy or unsure students a reason to walk through a door they would have walked past.

The second reason is that activities teach skills that a classroom can't. In math class I mostly work alone and my grade depends on me. On a soccer team, a debate squad, or student council, other people depend on me. If the treasurer of student council forgets to count the money from a bake sale, the whole dance budget is in trouble. Students learn to show up on time, finish what they promised, and work with people they didn't pick as friends. Colleges and employers say they want these habits, and a requirement makes sure every student gets practice.

Some people say students are already too busy, especially ones who have jobs or take care of younger brothers and sisters. That is a fair point, and the principal should deal with it. The rule says "at least one" activity, not three, and the school can keep the choices wide and flexible. A student could join a club that meets once a week, help in the school garden, or work on the stage crew for the spring play. A student with a part-time job could count a few hours of tutoring younger kids. One activity does not take over a schedule, and for most students it is a break from studying instead of extra work.

A third reason is that activities make the school feel like a community. Our school has over a thousand students, and it is easy to spend four years knowing only the kids in your classes. When students join the same band, service club, or track team, they meet people from other grades and other groups. I have seen seniors teach freshmen how to do a relay handoff, and I have seen friendships start between people who would never have talked in the hallway. Those connections make the building friendlier and help students feel like they belong, and students who feel like they belong usually come to school more and care more about their grades.

In the end, school is not only about passing tests. It is about growing into capable, curious people. Students spend most of their day at school, so it makes sense to use some of that time to help them find what they are good at. An extracurricular requirement is a small step toward that. It asks each student for a few hours a week and gives back confidence, teamwork, and friends. I urge my classmates to support the principal's decision, and I think a lot of them will be glad they did once they find the activity they didn't know they would love.
