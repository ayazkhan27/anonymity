Summer projects exist for a pretty simple reason. A break of ten or twelve weeks can make students forget a lot of what they learned during the year. Teachers call this the summer slide, and schools try to fix it by giving out work to keep kids thinking. The harder question is who should decide what that work looks like. I believe summer projects should be designed by the students, within a frame the teacher provides. When students pick the question they want to chase, the learning is more likely to stick after the break, and it is more likely to be real learning instead of a pile of busywork.

The strongest argument for student design is motivation. Summer is the one time of year when no bell rings and no teacher stands at the front of the room. Anything a student finishes then gets finished because the student chose to do it. A teacher-made packet of forty math problems asks a fifteen-year-old to give up beach days for work that feels exactly like a Tuesday worksheet, and most students respond the only way they can. They rush through it the night before school starts, or they copy answers from a friend. A project the student came up with works differently. A boy who loves basketball and decides to study how shooting angles affect accuracy will measure, record, and recalculate without anyone nagging him, because he actually wants to know the answer. Effort that comes from curiosity lasts longer than effort that comes from a deadline.

Student-designed projects also teach skills that teacher-designed ones can't. Choosing a topic, deciding what counts as evidence, making a schedule, and finishing without anyone watching are the habits of independent adults. A teacher-designed project already has the hardest part done, which is deciding what to do. The student just carries it out. When a student has to plan the project herself, she practices the whole cycle of learning. For example, a girl who is bothered by litter in her neighborhood park could design a project where she surveys the trash for six weeks, sorts it by type, and writes a proposal to the city asking for more recycling bins. She uses science, math, writing, and civic reasoning, and she does it in a way no single worksheet could script. Because she built it, she understands why each step matters.

Critics reasonably worry that students left to design their own work will choose something easy, or nothing at all. A student might say he will "watch documentaries" and call it research. This concern is real, but it is an argument for guidance, not for taking control away from students. The best setup is a partnership. Before school lets out, the teacher sets the requirements. The project has to include a question, a method, a record of progress, and a final product shared in the fall. Students then turn in a short proposal, and the teacher either approves it or sends it back to be fixed. This keeps the standards high while leaving the choice of subject with the student. A teacher can tell a vague idea from a workable one, and a quick talk in June can turn "watch documentaries" into "compare how three documentaries show the same historical event and write a review of which one is most accurate."

Another objection is fairness. Some students come from homes with books, computers, and parents who can help, while others don't. A teacher-designed project at least gives everyone the same task. But equal tasks do not give equal results, since the student with more resources still finishes faster and does it better. Student design can actually shrink the gap, because a kid can build a project out of what is available to her. A student without internet access can interview her grandmother about family history, write a short biography, and draw a map of the places the family has lived. That project needs a notebook and a conversation, not a subscription. Teachers can make things more even by handing out a list of free options and by letting the fall presentation take many forms, like an essay, a model, a video, or a talk.

Finally, student-designed projects make the first week back in September better for everyone. Instead of collecting a stack of identical packets, a teacher gets a room full of different discoveries. Students have something real to share, and classmates learn from each other. Listening to a peer explain what she found out about the park litter is way more interesting than reading a pile of answers to the same twenty questions.

Summer should keep students learning, but it should also let them enjoy learning. Teachers know what a good project requires, and students know what interests them. If teachers set the standards and students set the topics, schools can get both rigor and enthusiasm. That combination is what turns a required assignment into the kind of work students might do even if nobody asked them to.
