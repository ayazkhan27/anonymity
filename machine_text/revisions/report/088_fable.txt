One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When our principal announced that every student has to join at least one extracurricular activity, people started complaining before he even finished talking. They said they had no time, that school should be over at the last bell, and that you can't force somebody to have fun. I get it, because I said some of that stuff too. But after I thought about it more, I agree with the decision. One activity is not a lot to ask, and what you get out of it is worth more than the hour or two a week it takes.

My biggest reason is that a lot of kids will never try anything unless somebody pushes them. In sixth grade I signed up for nothing. I went home, did my homework, and watched videos until dinner. Then in seventh grade my mom made me join the school newspaper because she was sick of seeing me on the couch. I was mad about it for like two weeks. Then I wrote a story about why the cafeteria runs out of pizza every Friday, and kids I had never talked to before stopped me in the hall to ask about it. I found out I like interviewing people. I never would have known that if it was all up to me. The new rule does for every student what my mom did for me.

Activities also teach you things that classes don't. In math, if I don't do my work, the only thing that gets hurt is my grade. On yearbook it is different. If one person misses a deadline, forty pages go to the printer late and everybody has to deal with it. The student council treasurer has to stand in front of real classmates and explain why there isn't enough money for a dance and a field day both. A goalie who lets in three goals still has to show up to practice the next day. All of these are about other people counting on you and you counting on them. You can't learn that from a worksheet.

Another reason is that it helps kids feel like they belong. Our school has a few hundred students, and it can be lonely if the only people you know are the ones who sit by you. Activities mix up kids who would never meet any other way. In our chess club there are eighth graders teaching sixth graders how to play, and stage crew has kids from pretty much every lunch table. If you have even one group you belong to, then you have a reason to come to school on the days when classes are not enough of a reason.

I know there are arguments against this, and some of them are fair. Some students really are busy. They have to watch their little brothers and sisters, or they have a job, or their bus leaves at 3:10 and there is no other way home. The principal should take that seriously and not just ignore it. Some clubs could meet during lunch or advisory. Also, if a kid already plays in a town soccer league or volunteers at their church, that should count. If the rule works like that, it is really not asking for much. It is one activity, the student picks it, and there are dozens to pick from.

Other people say that making it required takes the fun out of it. I think that is backwards. Nobody is getting put on the debate team if they don't want to be. You still choose, and the list has robotics, art club, track, the literary magazine, and the garden committee on it. If you don't like anything on the list, you can suggest a new club. That is how our school got a cooking club.

School is supposed to get us ready for more than just tests. It is supposed to help us figure out what we are good at and how to work with other people. Requiring one activity means every student gets that chance, not only the confident ones. At the start of seventh grade I was mad that I got pushed into something. Now I am glad somebody did it.
