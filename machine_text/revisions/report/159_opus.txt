Every June we get handed the same packet on the last day of school. Reading log, math worksheets, sometimes a book report with a rubric stapled to the front. By August most people either finish it in one panicked weekend or just don't do it. I've done both. If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, then schools should let students design their own projects, and teachers should be more like guides than the ones who decide everything.

The biggest reason is motivation. A teacher-made project expects every student to care about the same thing at the same time, which basically never happens. Take a kid who loves baseball. He'd groan at a generic essay, but he would spend hours figuring out batting averages, tracking pitch speeds, and comparing players from different decades. That's actual statistics. Nobody would have to make him do it. When students pick the topic, the work stops being a chore that follows you home and turns into something that's yours. I honestly think stuff you learn because you're curious sticks way longer than stuff you learn to check off a list.

Second, designing a project is a skill by itself. In school almost every decision gets made for us: what to read, when it's due, how many pages. Adults have to set their own goals and plan how to get there all the time. A summer project is a low-stakes way to practice that. Say a girl decides to grow a small vegetable garden. She has to research what grows in her climate, budget for seeds and soil, remember to water on a schedule, and figure out why her tomatoes split. A worksheet does not teach that.

Third, students know what they actually have to work with during the summer. Every family's summer is different. One student spends July on her grandparents' farm. Another works part-time at a restaurant. Another watches his little brothers every single day while his parents are at work. One assignment can't fit all of those lives, but a project the student designs can. The restaurant worker could study how the kitchen keeps food costs down. The babysitter could write and draw a picture book for the kids he watches.

Some people will say that without teachers designing the project, students will pick something way too easy or quit halfway through. That's a fair point. But it's an argument for having structure, not for teachers making every choice. Schools could have students turn in a short proposal before summer starts that explains what they're doing, what they want to learn, and how they'll show it. Teachers can approve it, suggest changes, and check in once or twice over email. Then at the end students present to the class, so there's accountability and everyone gets to hear about each other's summers.

Summer is one of the only times we actually get to follow our own interests. Schools should use that instead of filling it with another packet. When students design their own projects, they learn the material, and they also learn how to teach themselves.
