Summer projects exist for a good reason. Anyone who has watched a classroom in September knows that two and a half months away from school can leave a student rusty in math, rusty in writing, and rusty in the habit of sitting down to think. But who designs those projects matters just as much as whether we assign them. I believe summer projects should be student-designed, with teacher guidance at the start and at the end.

The strongest argument for student design is motivation. A summer assignment competes with swimming pools, part-time jobs, family trips, and a hundred other things that feel more urgent. A teacher-made packet of twenty worksheets rarely wins that contest. It shows up as an obligation, and students respond to obligations by doing the minimum, usually on the last weekend of August. A project a student picked themselves has a better chance, because the student already cares about the answer. A girl who loves baking can spend the summer testing how altitude, humidity, and oven temperature change how bread rises, and she is doing real chemistry without anyone telling her that. A boy who wants to build a skateboard ramp has to figure out angles, estimate the cost of lumber, and write a budget. The learning is real because the goal is real.

Student design also teaches something a worksheet can't, which is how to plan. In college and at work, nobody hands you a list of problems with an answer key. You have to decide what to do, break it into steps, set deadlines, and notice when you are falling behind. Summer is a good time to practice that, because the stakes are low. If a student sets a goal that is too big and has to scale back, they learned something useful about how long things take, and all they lost was a few weeks of vacation. With a teacher-designed project, the student can hand all of that thinking to someone else.

The obvious objection is that students will pick easy projects, or pick nothing at all. That is a fair worry, and it is why the teacher still has a job here. A good system would have each student turn in a short proposal in June that explains what they plan to do, what they hope to learn, and how they will show the result. The teacher can approve it, push for more depth, or suggest a link to next year's curriculum. A student who says they will "watch documentaries" can be asked to write a review comparing three of them. A student who says they will read a book can be asked to pick one harder than what they usually read. Then in the fall, the student presents the finished work to the class. The proposal and the presentation let the teacher keep standards up without taking the project away from the student.

Some people will say that teacher-designed projects make sure everyone practices the same basic skills. But a summer assignment is a pretty bad tool for that anyway. Skills like fractions, grammar, or reading comprehension are better rebuilt in the first weeks of school, when a teacher can actually see who needs help. Summer should do something school can't, which is let a young person chase a question just because they want to know the answer.

If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, then the best project is the one a student will actually finish and care about. Letting students design their own, with a teacher checking the plan and the result, protects both rigor and interest. It also sends the message that learning doesn't stop when the bell rings, because it belongs to the learner.
