Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June, a lot of students carry home a packet along with their report card. Inside is the summer project, the assignment that is supposed to keep our brains working through ten weeks of vacation. I understand why schools do this. Teachers always talk about the "summer slide," where students forget part of the reading and math they learned by the time September comes. So I am not arguing against summer projects. The real question is who should decide what the project is. I think students should design their own summer projects, with teachers setting a few clear requirements and approving the plan before school lets out.

The biggest reason is motivation. During the school year a teacher can remind you, check in, and take points off if you fall behind. In July nobody is doing that. The only thing that gets a project done well, instead of thrown together the night before school starts, is whether the student actually cares about it. A project the teacher designs is the same for everybody, so it doesn't really fit anybody. One summer I was assigned a packet of thirty reading response worksheets. I did all of them in two days at the end of August, basically copying the same format from one page to the next, and a week later I could not have told you what any of the books were about. Compare that to a friend of mine who got to pick her own topic for a science class. She spent the summer testing which homemade fertilizer grew the tallest tomato plants in her backyard. She measured them every three days and came back in September with a notebook full of numbers and a strong opinion about compost. Nobody had to nag her, because the project was hers.

Student-designed projects also fit what summer is really like. We do not all spend the break the same way. One student works thirty hours a week at a grocery store. Another one stays with grandparents in a different state, and another has to watch younger brothers and sisters every day. One assignment written in May can't account for all of that. But a student who designs the project can build it around the summer they are actually going to have. The student with the grocery job could keep track of how prices change from June to August and try to explain why. The one visiting grandparents could record interviews about what the town was like fifty years ago and turn them into a family history. Those projects still take reading, writing, math, and research. They only work because they come out of a situation the teacher could not have known about.

A third reason is that designing a project is a skill by itself. You have to choose a question, figure out how to answer it, guess how long each step will take, and change the plan when something goes wrong. Those are the things adults keep saying people my age can't do. A teacher-designed project does all of that thinking for us and leaves only the work part. If the student has to plan it, then the planning is part of what we learn.

The obvious objection is that students who are left alone will pick something easy. Honestly, some would. Somebody is going to propose watching a TV series and writing a paragraph about it. Another concern is that teachers know what skills the next grade needs and students don't. Both of these are fair points, but they are reasons for teachers to guide the project, not to take it away from students completely. The teacher can require that every project has a certain amount of reading, a written piece of a set length, and some connection to next year's classes. Students would turn in a one page proposal in May, and the teacher would send the weak ones back to be fixed. That way the teacher protects the quality and the student still owns the project.

Summer projects exist because schools want learning to keep going when the building is closed. But learning with no supervision has to come from the learner. A project that a student chose, planned, and shaped around their own summer is much more likely to be done with care and remembered afterward than a packet handed to the whole class. Schools should set the standards and then let students design the work.
