I agree with the principal's decision that every student should join at least one extracurricular activity. Some kids will complain at first, and a few will say their afternoons are already full. But school is supposed to get us ready for more than tests, and a required activity gives everyone a chance to learn things you don't get in a classroom.

The main reason is that these activities teach skills that grades don't show. Take the yearbook. To finish one book, the team has to plan the pages, take and sort photos, write captions, and get everything to the printer on time. If one person falls behind, the whole book is late. A student who does that for a year learns to manage time, take advice from an editor, and count on classmates while they count on you. Student council is the same way. A member who helps put on the fall dance has to make a budget, get teachers to help, and deal with complaints when the music is too loud. Those are the same skills employers say they want, and most of us hardly practice them in a normal school day.

Another reason is that clubs and teams help students find where they fit in. Every school has quiet kids who come in, sit through class, and leave without really knowing anyone. I have seen this happen. A boy in my grade hardly said a word until he joined cross-country. He was not the fastest runner, but people welcomed him on the long bus rides and at team dinners, and by spring he was yelling for teammates he had never met the fall before. Belonging to something makes you want to come to school. I have read that students who are part of a club or team are less likely to drop out. If the rule helps even a few lonely students find a group, it is already worth it.

Some people say a requirement takes away choice. They think students should only join what they really care about, and that forced members will be bored and not try. That is a fair point, but the rule still leaves lots of choice. The principal did not say everyone has to play football. The list includes sports, yearbook, and student council, and the school could add chess club, drama, band, a recycling committee, or robotics. Someone who hates competing can paint sets for the spring play. Someone who loves computers can run the school website. The rule only says you have to pick something. Picking from many options is still a real choice, and it pushes students to try things they might never think about. A lot of us don't know what we like until we try it.

Time is another worry. Some students have jobs, watch their little brothers and sisters, or have a lot of homework. A good policy should think about that. The school can offer activities that meet at lunch or just once a week, and it can let a part-time job or family duties count in special cases. That way nobody gets left out just because they are busy. Being flexible keeps the rule fair without ruining the point of it. Even a busy student gets something out of one small commitment, because it gives them a routine with other people and something to look forward to.

Last, the habit matters after high school too. Adults who volunteer, coach a kids' team, or sit on a neighborhood board usually started joining things when they were young. Learning early that you can help a group, and that the group is better because of you, prepares students to be good citizens. A school that asks everyone to practice that is investing in the community they will lead someday.

For these reasons, I support the principal's decision. Extracurricular activities teach teamwork and responsibility, help lonely students connect, and let everyone find a talent. With good options and some flexibility, asking each student to do one activity is a small thing to ask for a big reward. I think the school should keep the rule.
