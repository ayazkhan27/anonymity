Summer Projects Should Be Student-Designed

Every June, students walk out of school with a long stretch of free time and, in a lot of districts, a packet of assignments meant to keep their brains from going soft. The goal makes sense. Research on the "summer slide" shows that students can lose a good part of their math and reading skills over the break. But who designs the work matters a lot. I think summer projects should be student-designed, with teachers acting as guides, because when students own the work they put in more effort, learn more for real, and pick up skills a worksheet never could.

The biggest reason is motivation. A teacher-designed project shows up in the middle of summer as one more thing to do, and students treat it that way. They put it off until August, copy answers from a friend, or rush through it the night before school starts. None of it belongs to them. When students pick their own topics, that changes. A student who loves basketball might spend the summer looking at shooting percentages and building a model that predicts which players will improve the most. That same student, handed a generic packet of probability problems, would probably do the bare minimum. Interest doesn't run out the way patience does, and student-designed projects use it directly.

Another reason is that designing a project is a lesson all by itself. Teacher-designed assignments hide the hardest part of real work, which is deciding what to do. Someone already picked the question, the format, and the deadline. A student who has to write a proposal has to take a vague interest and narrow it into a question they can actually answer, figure out how long each stage will take, and plan for the problems that will come up. Those are the habits that college professors and employers say young people are missing. A girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden and keep a record of it has to budget, research soil, write down observations, and adjust when the tomatoes die. She learns science, but she also learns to manage her own time, and no packet teaches that.

Some people say students left alone will pick easy projects or nothing at all. That is a fair worry, but it is an argument for structure, not for the teacher controlling everything. Schools can require a short proposal in June that the teacher reviews and approves, with a few check-ins during the summer and a presentation in the fall. The teacher sets the standard, like requiring research, a written reflection, and some proof of steady effort, while the student picks the subject. A teacher can also push a weak idea toward something better. A student who wants to "watch documentaries" could be asked to compare how three films show the same historical event and write about the differences. The teacher's experience makes the work better without taking it away from the student.

Another objection is fairness. If every student gets the same assignment, grading is simple and equal. But being the same is not the same as being fair. Students come back from summer with very different resources. One might have a library card and a quiet house, while another works a part-time job and watches younger siblings. An assignment that assumes you can get to museums, travel, or use a computer favors students who already have advantages. A flexible project lets each person build something that fits their life. The student with the job could interview coworkers about what they do and write an essay on the local economy, which only takes a notebook and some curiosity.

Finally, think about what students actually remember. Hardly anyone remembers a worksheet from the summer after sixth grade. A lot of people remember the time they built a birdhouse, wrote a short story, or taught themselves to code a simple game. Those things stick because the person made them. A student who finishes a project they chose goes back to school with something to show and the confidence of having shaped it.

Summer learning matters, and schools are right to protect it. The best way to do that is to trust students to design the work, hold them accountable with proposals and presentations, and let teachers coach instead of command. When the learning belongs to the student, it lasts well past September.
