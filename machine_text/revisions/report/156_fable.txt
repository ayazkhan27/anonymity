Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June, a packet goes home in my cousin's backpack. It has a reading list of three novels, a worksheet for each one, and a rubric stapled to the back. Every August, the night before school starts, he fills in the worksheets at the kitchen table using chapter summaries he found online. He gets full credit. He has not learned anything, except maybe how fast he can write when he is panicking. I think his school has the right goal and the wrong way of getting there. Summer projects should be designed by students, with teachers setting the limits and checking the plan.

The main reason is that people work harder on things they chose. During the school year, a student can be pushed through an assignment he does not care about because the teacher is standing there five days a week. In the summer nobody is standing there. The only thing that keeps a project going through July is the student's own interest. Say a girl loves baking and decides to test how different amounts of baking soda change a cookie. She is going to run that experiment six or seven times, because she wants to eat the results and because she really does want to know which batch will come out flat. She will measure, write things down, compare, and explain what happened. That is basically the scientific method, and no one had to force her. Give the same girl a packet on the phases of the moon and she will do exactly what my cousin does.

A second reason is that designing the project is a skill by itself. When a teacher hands over a finished assignment, the hardest thinking has already been done. The teacher chose the question, decided what counts as an answer, and figured out how long each part should take. Students almost never get to practice those steps, and then we get to college or a job and are expected to already know them. A student who plans to interview her grandfather about leaving Vietnam has to decide what to ask, how to record it, and what to do when he does not want to talk about a certain year. A student who wants to build a birdhouse has to price the wood and then find out he measured wrong. Those problems are frustrating, but I think they teach more than a rubric does.

Third, summers are not equal. A teacher-designed project assumes every student has the same summer, and we do not. One student is at a lake for two months. Another is watching her little brothers every day while her parents work. Another has a job at a grocery store. A project that requires visiting a museum or having fast internet is easy for some students and close to impossible for others. When students design their own, they can build around the life they actually have. The girl babysitting her brothers can try teaching the four-year-old to read and keep track of what works. The boy at the grocery store can write down prices for ten weeks and graph them.

The strongest argument on the other side is that some students will pick something too easy, or will pick nothing at all. That is a real problem, and it is why I do not think teachers should be taken out of the process. Before school lets out, each student should turn in a one-page proposal with the question, the plan, and what the final product will be. The teacher approves it or sends it back with comments. The teacher can also require that every project include some reading, some writing, and some kind of evidence. That way the standards still belong to the teacher, but the subject belongs to the student. Also, a teacher who reads thirty proposals in June gets thirty different projects in September instead of thirty copies of the same worksheet, which is probably more interesting for her too.

The purpose of a summer project is to keep students learning when no one is making them. A packet does not do that, at least not for my cousin. A question the student actually wants answered can.
