Summer projects should be designed by students. Teachers already shape the school year, and a summer assignment handed down from above often turns into a task to finish instead of a reason to learn. When students pick their own projects, they care about them.

Take a student who loves baseball but dreads her required reading list. If she designs a project comparing batting statistics across decades, she has to learn some statistics, research baseball history, and write clearly to explain what she found. A generic book report, by contrast, lets her skim the book and forget it by September.

Student design does not mean no guidance. Teachers can set requirements, such as minimum hours, a final product, and a short proposal for approval. That structure keeps projects rigorous while letting students own their learning.

Ownership also builds the skill that matters most after school: deciding what to learn and how to pursue it. A teacher-designed project teaches compliance; a student-designed one teaches independence.
