When people ask for advice, they usually hear one answer and think that answer must be right. My friend says to take the job, and my mom says to stay where I am, and after a while the decision feels finished even though nobody really looked at it. I think asking for more than one opinion is smart because it turns one view into a comparison, and comparing things is how people make better choices.

Every advisor sees a problem through their own lens. Their experience, their values, and their past mistakes all change what they notice. A former teacher might think about whether a graduate program will look good on a resume. A current grad student might think about how many hours the program really takes and whether the stipend pays rent. Neither one is wrong, but both are incomplete. If you hear both, you can ask a better question, like: what does the program cost in time and money, and what does it give back in career terms?

Multiple opinions also show where advisors disagree, and those disagreements are often the most helpful part. Say someone is thinking about buying a used car. One mechanic says the engine looks good, but another one notices a worn timing belt that would cost about a thousand dollars to replace. That disagreement tells the buyer exactly where to look more closely. If they only asked one mechanic, they would think the car was fine.

There is also a protection benefit. Advice from one person can carry that person's bias, and nobody notices. A salesperson who wants to sell an expensive laptop obviously has a reason to push it, but a less obvious bias can come from a friend who always likes stability and will talk you out of anything risky. When several people with different reasons give their opinions, the biases tend to cancel each other out. Then you can tell which advice is based on facts and which is based on feelings.

Of course, more opinions are not always better. A person can collect so many opinions that they get stuck, or they can just pick the answer they already wanted to hear. The goal is not to count votes. The goal is to understand why each person thinks what they think and then compare those reasons to what you care about. Say someone is picking a college. Their parent cares about prestige, their counselor cares about cost, and a recent graduate wishes they had not gone so far from home. The useful step is figuring out which of those things matters most for that student, instead of just letting the loudest voice win.

Good advice-seeking also means picking advisors who know different things. If you ask three people who all come from the same background, you will probably get the same answer three times and not learn much. Asking one person who has done the thing before, one person who works in that field, and one person who knows you really well usually gives you a fuller picture.

In the end, the decision still belongs to the person who has to live with it. Multiple opinions do not take that responsibility away. What they do give you is a wider view of your options, a clearer idea of the trade-offs, and a better chance of noticing the detail you would have missed. That is why getting more than one voice so often leads to a better choice.
