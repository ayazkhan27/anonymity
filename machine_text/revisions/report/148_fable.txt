Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June my cousin used to come home with the same packet. It was forty pages of reading passages and math review, stapled at the corner, due the first day of school. Every August, usually in the last four days before it was due, he filled it out at the kitchen table and complained the whole time. He finished it every year, and I doubt he could have told you one thing that was in it by October. That packet is the main reason I think summer projects should be designed by students, with teachers setting the limits but not the topic.

The biggest reason is pretty simple. Summer work only gets done if the student decides to do it. During the school year a teacher can check in every day, answer questions, and notice when somebody is falling behind. In July nobody is watching. A project that a student picked has a much better chance of lasting ten weeks with no teacher around than one that was just handed to them. For example, a girl who loves cooking could spend the summer testing what happens to a batch of cookies when she changes the amount of baking soda. She could write down the results each time and then write up what she found. She would be practicing measurement, ratios, and the scientific method, and she would be doing it because she actually wanted to know the answer. If you gave that same girl a worksheet on ratios, she would do it the night before it was due.

Designing the project also teaches something a teacher-made assignment can't, which is how to plan. Picking a topic, deciding what the final product should look like, and breaking the work into steps are things students almost never have to do, because the assignment sheet already does it for them. Colleges and jobs expect people to handle tasks that don't come with step by step directions. A summer project is a safe place to try that. Say a student with no coding experience decides to build a working app, finds out it is way too big, and has to cut it down to a simple quiz game. That is a real lesson about how much you can take on, and all it costs is a few frustrating weeks.

There is also a fairness problem that I think people forget about. Students do not all have the same summer. Some travel, some work thirty hours a week, some watch their younger siblings all day, and some don't have reliable internet at home. One project designed by a teacher assumes one kind of summer. But a student who works at his family's restaurant could design a project where he tracks the busiest hours and then proposes a better schedule for the staff. That project fits into the life he already has. A required museum visit or an online research assignment might not.

The usual argument against this is that students will pick something easy, or they won't cover what the school needs them to learn. That is a fair worry, and it is why teachers should still be part of it. A teacher can require a written proposal in May and list the skills the project has to show, like research from at least three sources or a certain number of pages of writing. The teacher can also hand out the rubric ahead of time. A proposal to "watch movies all summer" gets sent back. A proposal to compare three movies to the novels they were based on gets approved. The teacher protects the standards and the student comes up with the idea.

Teachers know what students need to learn, but students know what they will actually do in the middle of July. A summer project only works when you have both, and letting students design the project inside rules the teacher sets is the best way I can think of to get there.
