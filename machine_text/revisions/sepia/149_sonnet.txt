Summer projects are a good idea. Anybody who has seen a classroom in September knows what two and a half months off can do. Kids come back rusty at math, rusty at writing, and rusty at sitting down to think. But who designs the project matters as much as whether we assign one. I think summer projects should be designed by the students, with the teacher helping at the start and at the end.

The biggest reason is motivation. A summer assignment has to compete with pools, jobs, family trips, and a lot of other stuff that feels more important. A packet of twenty worksheets from the teacher almost never wins. It shows up as an obligation, and when students get an obligation they do the minimum, usually the last weekend of August. A project the student picked has a better shot because they already want the answer. Say a girl who loves baking spends the summer testing how altitude, humidity, and oven temperature change how bread rises. She's doing real chemistry and nobody had to tell her that. Or a boy wants to build a skateboard ramp. He has to figure out angles, guess the cost of lumber, and write a budget. The learning counts because the goal is real.

Designing it also teaches something a worksheet can't, which is planning. In college or at a job nobody hands you a list of problems with an answer key. You have to decide what to do, break it into steps, set deadlines, and notice when you're falling behind. Summer is a good time to practice this because not much is at stake. If a student sets a goal that's too big and has to cut it back, they learned how long things really take them, and all they lost was a few weeks of vacation. With a teacher-designed project the student just hands all that thinking off to someone else.

The obvious objection is that students will pick easy projects or pick nothing. That's a fair worry, and it's why the teacher still matters. In June each student would turn in a short proposal that says what they plan to do, what they hope to learn, and how they'll show the result. The teacher can approve it, ask for more depth, or connect it to what's coming up next year. If a student says they'll watch documentaries, the teacher can ask for a review comparing three of them. If someone says they'll read a book, they can be told to pick one harder than what they usually read. Then in the fall the student presents the finished work to the class. The proposal and the presentation let the teacher keep the standards up without taking the project away from the student.

Some people say teacher-designed projects make sure everybody practices the same basic skills. But a summer assignment isn't a good tool for that anyway. Skills like fractions, grammar, and reading comprehension are better rebuilt in the first weeks of school, when the teacher can actually see who needs help. Summer should do what school can't, which is let a young person chase a question just because they want to.

So if the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, the best project is the one a student will actually finish and care about. Letting students design their own, with a teacher checking the plan at the start and the result at the end, protects both the rigor and the interest. It also sends the message that learning doesn't stop when the bell rings, because it belongs to the person doing it. I really do think that's the better deal for everyone, because a project nobody finishes doesn't teach anything. That's the whole point of doing summer projects.
