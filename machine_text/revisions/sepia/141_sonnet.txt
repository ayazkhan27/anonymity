Every summer, students get ten or twelve weeks off, and a lot of what they learned during the year disappears. People call this the summer slide, and schools try to stop it by giving out summer work. The real question is who should plan that work. I think summer projects should be designed by the students, as long as the teacher sets the rules. If students pick the question they want to answer, they will remember more over the break, and the project will actually be learning instead of busywork.

The biggest reason is motivation. In summer there is no bell and no teacher at the front of the room, so anything a student does is something they chose to do. If a teacher hands out a packet of forty math problems, a fifteen-year-old has to give up beach days for something that feels exactly like a Tuesday worksheet. Most kids do what you would expect. They rush it the night before school starts or copy answers from a friend. A project the student came up with works differently. Say a boy loves basketball and wants to find out how shooting angles change accuracy. He will measure and record and do the math again without anybody nagging him, because he wants the answer. Work that comes from being curious lasts longer than work that comes from a deadline.

Student-designed projects also teach things a teacher-designed one cannot. Picking a topic, deciding what counts as proof, making a schedule, and finishing with nobody watching are what independent adults do. When the teacher designs the project, the hardest part is already done, which is deciding what to do, and the student just follows steps. When the student plans it herself, she goes through the whole process of learning. For example, a girl who is bothered by litter in her neighborhood park could survey the trash for six weeks, sort it by type, and write a proposal asking the city for more recycling bins. That uses science, math, writing, and thinking about her community all at once, and no single worksheet could do that. Since she built the project, she understands why each step matters.

Some people worry that students who design their own work will pick something easy, or nothing at all. A kid might say he will "watch documentaries" and call it research. That is a fair concern, but it is a reason to give guidance, not a reason to take the choice away. The best way is for the teacher and student to work together. Before school lets out, the teacher sets the requirements. The project needs a question, a method, a record of progress, and a final product that gets shared in the fall. Then each student turns in a short proposal, and the teacher either approves it or sends it back to be fixed. That keeps the work rigorous while the student still chooses the subject. A teacher can tell a vague idea from one that will work, and a quick talk in June can change "watch documentaries" into "compare how three documentaries show the same historical event and write a review of which one is most accurate."

Another objection is fairness. Some students have books, computers, and parents who can help at home, and some do not. If the teacher assigns one project, at least everyone gets the same task. But the same task does not give the same results, because the student with more resources still finishes faster and does it better. Student design can actually close the gap, since a kid can build a project out of whatever she has. A student with no internet could interview her grandmother about the family's history, write a short biography, and draw a map of the places the family has lived. All that takes is a notebook and a conversation, not a subscription. Teachers can make it even fairer by handing out a list of free options and letting students present in the fall in different ways, like an essay, a model, a video, or a talk.

Finally, student-designed projects make the first week back in September better for everybody. Instead of collecting a pile of packets that all look the same, the teacher gets a room full of different discoveries. Students have something real to share, and classmates learn from each other. Hearing a classmate explain what she found out about the litter in the park is a lot more interesting than reading a stack of answers to the same twenty questions.

Summer should keep students learning, but it should also let them enjoy it. Teachers know what a good project needs, and students know what they care about. If teachers set the standards and students pick the topics, schools get both hard work and real interest. That is what makes a required assignment into something students might do even if nobody asked them to.
