One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When our principal said that every student has to join at least one extracurricular activity, kids started complaining before the announcement was even over. They said they don't have time. They said school should be done when the last bell rings and that you can't force somebody to have fun. I get it, because I said some of that stuff too. But I thought about it more and now I agree with the decision. One activity is not a lot to ask. It takes maybe an hour or two a week, and I think students get way more out of it than that.

My biggest reason is that a lot of kids will never try anything unless somebody pushes them. I know because I was one of them. In sixth grade I didn't sign up for a single thing. I went home, did my homework, and watched videos until dinner. Then in seventh grade my mom made me join the school newspaper because she was tired of seeing me on the couch. I was annoyed about it for around two weeks. After that I wrote a story about why the cafeteria ran out of pizza every Friday, and people I had never even talked to stopped me in the hall to talk about it. That's how I found out I like interviewing people. I never would have known if it was all up to me. The rule would do for every student what my mom did for me.

Activities also teach you things that classes don't. If I don't do my work in math, my grade goes down and nobody else gets hurt. On the yearbook staff it's different. If one person misses a deadline, forty pages go to the printer late and everybody pays for it. The student council treasurer has to explain to actual classmates why there isn't enough money for both a dance and a field day. A goalkeeper who lets in three goals still has to show up to practice the next afternoon. All of these are about depending on other people and having them depend on you. You can't get that from a worksheet.

Another reason is belonging. A school with several hundred students can be lonely if the only people you know are the ones who sit near you. Activities mix up students who would never meet any other way. In the chess club at our school, eighth graders teach sixth graders. The stage crew has kids from every lunch table. If a student has even one group to belong to, they have a reason to come to school on the days when classes aren't enough of a reason.

I know some people have real reasons to disagree, so I want to answer them. Some students really are busy. They watch their little brothers and sisters, or they have jobs, or their bus leaves at 3:10. The principal should take that seriously and make the rule flexible. Some clubs could meet during lunch or advisory. Also, if a student already plays in a town soccer league or volunteers at a church, that should count. If the school does those things, the rule asks for very little. It's one activity, the student picks it, and there are dozens to pick from.

Other people say that forcing students takes the fun out of it. I think that's backwards. Nobody is getting put on the debate team when they don't want to be. You still choose, and the list has robotics, art club, track, the literary magazine, and the garden committee on it. If nothing on the list fits you, you can suggest something new. That is how our school got a cooking club.

School is supposed to get us ready for more than tests. It's supposed to help us figure out what we're good at and how to work with other people. If one activity is required, then every student gets that chance, even the ones who aren't confident. At the start of seventh grade I was mad that I got pushed into something. Now I'm glad somebody pushed me.
