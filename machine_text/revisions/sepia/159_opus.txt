Every June students leave school with the same summer packet. There's a reading log, some math worksheets, and maybe a book report with the rubric stapled on the front. By August most kids either rush through the whole thing in one weekend or just don't do it. If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, then schools should let students design their own projects and have teachers guide them instead of writing the assignment for them.

The biggest reason is motivation. When a teacher makes the project, everyone is supposed to care about the same thing at the same time, and that pretty much never happens. Say a student loves baseball. He might groan at a generic essay, but he would spend hours figuring out batting averages, tracking pitch speeds, and comparing players from different decades. That's actual statistics, and he'd do it because he wants to. When students pick the topic, the project stops feeling like a chore that followed them home and starts feeling like it's theirs. Stuff you learn because you're curious sticks a lot longer than stuff you learn to check off a list.

Second, planning a project is a skill on its own, and students barely get to practice it. In school almost every decision is made for us: what we read, when it's due, how long it has to be. Adults have to set their own goals and figure out how to reach them all the time. A summer project is a low-stakes way to practice that. For example, a girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden has to look up what plants grow in her climate, budget for seeds and soil, stick to a watering schedule, and figure out what went wrong when her tomatoes split open. A worksheet can't teach you that kind of planning and problem solving.

Third, when students design their projects they can use what they actually have. Everybody's summer is different. One kid spends July on their grandparent's farm, another works part time at a restaurant, and another has to babysit their little siblings every day. One assignment from a teacher can't fit all of those lives, but a project the student designs can. The kid at the restaurant could study how the kitchen keeps track of food costs. The babysitter could write and illustrate a picture book for the kids she watches.

Some people will say that if teachers don't design the projects, students will pick something way too easy or give up halfway through. That's a fair point, but it means there should be structure, not that teachers have to make every choice. Schools could have students turn in a short proposal before summer starts that says what they're doing, what they want to learn, and how they'll show it. Teachers could approve it or suggest changes, and then check in once or twice over email. At the end students could present to the class, which keeps them accountable and lets everyone learn from each other's summers.

Summer is one of the only times students actually get to follow their own interests. Schools should use that freedom instead of filling it up with another packet. When students design their own projects they still learn the material, and they also learn how to teach themselves.
