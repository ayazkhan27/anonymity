Summer projects should be student-designed. Teachers already shape the school year, and a summer assignment handed down from above often turns into a task to finish instead of a reason to learn. When students pick their own projects, they get curious about them for real.

Take a student who loves baseball but dreads her required reading list. If she designs a project comparing batting stats across decades, she has to learn statistics, research history, and write clearly to explain what she found. Give her a generic book report instead, and she'll skim the book and forget it by September.

That doesn't mean no guidance. Teachers can set requirements like a minimum time commitment, a deliverable, and a short proposal for approval. That keeps projects rigorous while students still own their learning.

Ownership also builds the skill that matters most after school: deciding what to learn and how to go after it. A summer project a teacher designs teaches compliance. One a student designs teaches independence.
