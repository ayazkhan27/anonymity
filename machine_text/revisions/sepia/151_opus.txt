Every June we get handed a summer packet, and every August a lot of us finish it in one rushed weekend before school starts. Summer projects are supposed to keep our brains working over break, but whether they actually do depends on how they're set up. I think summer projects should be designed by the students themselves, with teachers giving clear guidelines and checkpoints along the way. When students get to pick what they study they work harder, they learn more, and they practice skills a worksheet can't teach.

The first reason is motivation. Summer is the only time of year when students have real freedom, so a project that ignores what they're interested in feels like a punishment for getting a vacation. A teacher-designed assignment, like reading three assigned novels and answering questions on each one, usually gets done at the last minute because nobody cares about it. Now think about a student who loves baseball and decides to track batting averages all summer, make graphs, and figure out which players got better. That kid is doing statistics without anyone making him, and he'll probably keep going after he's done the minimum. If students aren't interested, the learning just doesn't happen.

Second, designing a project is a skill in itself. In school the teacher usually gives you the question, the materials, and the due date. Adults don't really get instructions that clear. A student who has to choose a topic, plan out the steps, find resources, and manage their time for ten weeks is practicing the kind of independence college and jobs expect. My cousin decided one summer that she wanted to learn how to fix old bikes. She watched tutorials, bought parts on a budget, and kept notes on what didn't work. By September she had fixed four bikes and sold two of them, and she learned more about planning than any packet would have taught her.

Third, letting students design their projects respects the fact that everyone is different. One project for the whole class assumes we all have the same interests, the same resources, and the same kind of summer. One student might spend July babysitting younger siblings while another one is traveling or working a part-time job. If students design their own projects, each of them can find something that fits their actual life, whether that's writing a family history, starting a garden, or coding a simple game.

Some people will say that if you leave students on their own they'll pick something easy or just do nothing. That's a fair point, but it's a reason to add structure, not to take away the choice. Teachers could have students turn in a short proposal before school lets out, set a few standards for effort and evidence, and have everyone give a quick presentation in the fall. That way students still own their projects and teachers can still hold them accountable.

The point of a summer project is to keep students learning, not just to keep them busy. If students design their own work with some guidance from a teacher, they'll actually care about it, they'll get better at thinking on their own, and they'll come back in the fall with something they're proud to show.
