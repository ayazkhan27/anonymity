Who Should Design the Summer Project?

My cousin used to get the same packet every June. It was forty pages of reading passages and math review stapled at the corner, and it was due the first day of school. He always did it in the last four days of August at the kitchen table, and he complained the whole time. He finished it every year. I really doubt he remembered anything in it by October. Because of that packet, I think students should design their own summer projects, and teachers should set the limits for them.

My biggest reason is that summer work only gets done if the student decides to do it. During the school year a teacher sees you every day. They can answer questions and they notice when you're falling behind. In July nobody is checking. So a project that a student picked is a lot more likely to last ten weeks than one somebody else handed them. Say a girl loves cooking. She could spend the summer testing what happens to a batch of cookies when she changes the amount of baking soda, write down the results, and then write up what she found. That is measurement, ratios, and the scientific method, and she would be doing it because she actually wants to know the answer. If you gave that same girl a worksheet on ratios, she would do it the night before it was due.

Designing a project also teaches students how to plan, which a teacher's assignment can't really do. Normally the assignment sheet already tells you the topic, what the final product should look like, and how to split up the work. Students almost never have to figure those things out. But colleges and jobs expect people to handle open-ended tasks, and a summer project is a low-risk place to practice. Maybe a student with no coding experience decides to build a working app. That turns out to be way too big, so he has to cut it down to a simple quiz game. He still learned something real about how big a project should be, and all it cost him was a few frustrating weeks.

There is also a fairness problem that I think people forget about. Students don't all have the same summer. Some travel. Some work thirty hours a week, some watch their younger siblings all day, and some don't have reliable internet. When a teacher designs one project for everybody, it assumes everybody has one kind of summer. A required museum visit or an online research assignment might not be possible for some kids. But a student who works at a family restaurant could track the restaurant's busiest hours and propose a better staffing schedule. That project fits the life the student already has.

Some people will say that students will just pick something easy, or that they won't cover what the school needs them to learn. I think that is a fair worry, and it is the reason teachers still have to be part of this. A teacher can require a written proposal in May. The teacher can also list the skills the project has to show, like research from at least three sources or a certain amount of writing, and give out a rubric ahead of time. If someone proposes to "watch movies all summer," it gets sent back. If someone proposes to compare three film adaptations to the novels they came from, it gets approved. The student comes up with the idea and the teacher makes sure it meets the standards.

Teachers know what students need to learn. Students know what they will actually do in the middle of July. A summer project needs both of those things to work, so students should design the project and teachers should set the rules around it.
