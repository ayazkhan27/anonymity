Drivers shouldn't be allowed to use cell phones at all while they're driving. Phones are convenient, but convenience isn't worth a crash that could kill somebody.

Driving takes your full attention. A lot of people think hands-free calling is a safe middle ground, but the research doesn't back that up. There are three kinds of distraction, which are visual, manual, and cognitive. Hands-free gets rid of the manual one. Your mind is still on the conversation though. Drivers who are on a call miss their exits, don't see pedestrians, and are slower to react when brake lights come on. Looking at the road isn't the same thing as seeing it. Since the mental distraction is still there, a law that lets people go hands-free just makes drivers feel safer than they really are.

Texting is worse. If you read a message at highway speed, you travel the length of a football field with your eyes off the road. Nothing that shows up on a screen is that urgent. Most texts can wait ten minutes, and if it really can't wait, like an emergency, you can pull over first.

Some people say a total ban goes too far because phones also do navigation and music. There's an easy answer to that. You can pick a route and a playlist before you leave your driveway, and plenty of cars now have built-in systems made for use on the road. A phone that's mounted for directions and left alone is not the same as a phone somebody is actively using. A clear rule against handling the phone or using it for calls or messages is easier to enforce than a bunch of exceptions. An officer can see a phone being used without guessing what the driver is doing on it.

We already accept limits on how people drive, like speed limits and drunk driving laws, because the road is shared and one person's choices put many other people in danger. Phone use fits in with those. A call or a text is never worth somebody's life, so drivers should wait until the car is parked.
