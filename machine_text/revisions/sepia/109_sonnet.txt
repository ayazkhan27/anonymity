Drivers Should Not Use Cell Phones While Driving

Pretty much everyone has a cell phone now, and we pick it up dozens of times a day without even thinking about it. On a couch that's no big deal. In a car it is. I think drivers should not be allowed to use a handheld phone at all while they're driving, and the law should treat the phone as the danger it is.

The main problem is attention. Driving seems automatic but it isn't. You have to watch the car in front of you, check your mirrors, notice somebody standing at the curb, and guess what the other drivers are about to do. A phone uses the same part of your brain. Researchers talk about three kinds of distraction. Visual is when your eyes leave the road, manual is when your hands leave the wheel, and cognitive is when your mind leaves the job of driving. Texting does all three at once. If a driver looks at a message for five seconds on the highway, the car travels about the length of a football field and the driver never looked at the road. No new car feature fixes that.

The numbers show it too. Distracted driving kills thousands of people in the United States each year, and the real number is probably higher, since drivers hardly ever admit they were on their phones after a crash. A lot of the people who die weren't the distracted driver. They were passengers, bike riders, kids walking to school, or other drivers who were doing everything right. That's why I don't buy that this is about personal freedom. If someone wants to risk his own life, that's one thing. Risking strangers' lives is something else.

Some people say hands-free calling is safe enough and a law should only cover texting. That sounds fair, but the research doesn't back it up. Studies with driving simulators and real traffic data found that the conversation is itself the distraction, not only holding the phone. A driver in a deep phone call misses more traffic signals and is slower to react when someone brakes suddenly, even with both hands on the wheel and eyes straight ahead. The brain can't give itself fully to a conversation and the road at the same time. It flips back and forth, and every flip costs time a driver might not have. Hands-free fixes the hands problem but it doesn't fix the thinking problem.

Other people say we already talk to passengers and change the radio, so a phone is the same. It isn't. A passenger is in the car and usually stops talking when traffic gets tense. Someone on the phone can't see the intersection, so they keep talking. Changing the radio is quick and simple, but a phone has endless messages, notifications, and apps that are made to hold your attention. It's built to be hard to ignore, and that's exactly the wrong thing to have in a moving car.

Enforcement is a fair worry, because a law doesn't work if nobody follows it. But seat belt laws and drunk driving laws show that strict rules change what people do over time. When the penalties are real and people understand why, something that used to seem normal starts to feel irresponsible. A simple rule is also easier to enforce than a bunch of exceptions. If an officer sees a phone in a driver's hand, he doesn't have to guess if the driver was dialing, reading, or doing something that's allowed.

There should be a few reasonable exceptions. Someone with a real emergency, like reporting a crash or calling for help, should be able to use the phone, and setting up a navigation screen before the trip starts is fine. A driver can also just park and then use the phone. These exceptions are small and they don't change the main point, which is that driving isn't the time for calls, texts, or scrolling.

Nobody has to be reachable every minute of the day. Getting there a little late or letting a message wait costs almost nothing, but the other choice can cost a life. Drivers should put the phone away until the car is parked, and the law should make them do it.
