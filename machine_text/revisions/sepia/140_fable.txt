Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June a lot of students go home with a packet stapled to their report card. That's the summer project, the assignment that is supposed to keep us thinking through ten weeks of vacation. Schools assign them because of "summer slide." Research shows students can lose a month or more of reading and math progress between June and September, and I think that's a fair reason to assign something. What I don't agree with is the teacher deciding every detail of it. Students should design their own summer projects. Teachers should set a few clear requirements and approve each plan before school gets out.

The main reason is motivation. During the school year a teacher can remind you and check on you. In July nobody does. If a project gets done well, and not thrown together the night before school starts, it's because the student actually cares about it. A project the teacher wrote is the same for everybody, so it doesn't really fit anybody. One summer I was assigned a packet of thirty reading-response worksheets. I did all of them in two days at the end of August. I basically copied the format from one page to the next, and a week later I couldn't have told you what any of the books were about. A friend of mine had a science class where she got to pick her own topic. She tested which homemade fertilizer grew the tallest tomato plants in her backyard and measured them every three days all summer. In September she came back with a notebook full of data and a real opinion about compost. Nobody had to nag her, because it was her project.

Summers are also not the same for everyone, and a project the student designs can deal with that. One student works thirty hours a week at a grocery store. Another is living with grandparents in a different state. Somebody else has to watch younger siblings every day. A teacher writing one assignment in May can't plan for all of that, but the student can, since they know what their summer is actually going to look like. The one with the grocery job could track how prices change over the summer and explain why. The one staying with grandparents could record interviews about what the town was like fifty years ago and turn them into a family history. Those projects still take reading, writing, math, and research. They only work because they come out of situations a teacher never could have predicted.

Planning a project is also a skill that is worth learning. You have to choose a question, decide how to answer it, guess how long each step will take, and change the plan when something goes wrong. Colleges and employers say these are exactly the abilities young people are missing. When the teacher designs the project, the teacher does all of that thinking and the student is left with just the labor. If students have to plan the work themselves, then the planning is part of what they learn.

Some people would say that students left alone will just pick something easy, and some would. A student might propose watching a TV series and writing one paragraph about it. Teachers also know what skills the next grade requires, and students don't. I think both of these are fair points. But they are reasons for teachers to guide the projects. They aren't reasons to take the design away from students completely. The teacher can require that every project has a certain amount of reading, a written product of a set length, and some connection to next year's subjects. Students would turn in a one-page proposal in May, and the teacher would send the weak ones back to be revised. The teacher protects the quality that way, and the project still belongs to the student.

Schools give summer projects because they want learning to keep going when the building is closed. But when no one is supervising, the learner has to be the one driving it. A student who chose and planned a project around their own summer is a lot more likely to finish it with care and remember it afterward than one who got the same packet as the whole class. So schools should set the standards and then let students design the work.
