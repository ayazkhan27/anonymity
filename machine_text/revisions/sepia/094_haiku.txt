Every student should have to join at least one extracurricular activity. Some students will hate this rule. They think school is for classes, and they say a forced activity wastes time they could use for homework, a part-time job, or just sleeping. I get that. But I think the principal made the right call. Being required to join gives students a place to belong, teaches skills that classrooms usually don't reach, and sometimes shows people talents they didn't know they had.

The biggest benefit is belonging. A lot of students show up to high school feeling invisible. They sit in huge classes, eat lunch fast, and go home without talking to anybody outside their small group of friends. An activity can change that. Take a kid who joins the yearbook staff because it's the only elective left in his schedule. Over the year he learns how to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and argue with editors about page layouts. By spring he knows dozens of people from every grade, and the teachers who advise the staff have become mentors to him. If it wasn't required, he probably never would have walked into that darkroom.

Activities also teach things that tests don't measure. A kid on the debate team learns to research both sides of an argument and to talk when everyone is staring at him. A soccer player learns that one missed pass hurts the whole team, and that practicing on a cold Tuesday matters more than any one game. Student council members learn budgeting when they have to decide how to spend two thousand dollars from fundraising. They also learn to compromise when three classes want three different homecoming themes. You learn these lessons by doing them, and a requirement makes sure everybody gets the chance.

Critics say forcing people to join turns activities into chores. That's true for some students who get stuck in a club they hate. But the rule doesn't make anyone pick a certain one. It only asks students to try something. A kid who doesn't like sports can pick chess, robotics, or the literary magazine. A kid who gets nervous talking in front of people can join photography or the school garden. Most people don't know what they like until they try a few things, and the requirement makes sure that trying happens. Lots of adults can name one hobby that changed their life, and many started it only because they had to try something.

Finally, activities connect what we learn in class to the real world. Class can explain how the government works, but student council shows how a vote gets counted and how an idea turns into a rule. Class can talk about teamwork, but a band or a robotics team needs it every afternoon. These experiences get students ready for jobs, college, and being part of a community, where teamwork and initiative matter as much as grades.

Some people will say one rule can't fit every student. They're right, and that's exactly why the rule has to be broad. It should be wide enough that each student can find something that fits. The principal's decision understands that school should be more than handing out information. It should help students become confident, connected, and curious. So I agree that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity.
