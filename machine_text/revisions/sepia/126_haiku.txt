When people ask for advice, they usually hear one answer and think that answer is the right one. A friend says take the job, and a parent says stay where you are. Then the decision feels finished before anyone has really looked at it. I think getting more than one opinion helps because it gives you something to compare. Most good choices come from comparing.

Every advisor sees a situation through their own lens. Their experience, their values, and their past mistakes all change what they notice and what they think matters. A former teacher might focus on whether a graduate program will look good on a resume. A current grad student might focus on how many hours the program really takes and whether the stipend pays rent. Neither one is wrong, but both are incomplete. If you hear both, you can ask a better question: what does the program cost in time and money, and what does it give back in career terms?

Multiple opinions also show where people disagree, and those disagreements are often the most useful part. Say someone is thinking about buying a used car. One mechanic says the engine sounds good. Another mechanic finds a worn timing belt that would cost about a thousand dollars to replace. That disagreement tells the buyer exactly where to look harder. If the buyer had only asked the first mechanic, they would have walked away thinking the car was in good shape.

Advice from one person can also carry that person's bias, and nobody may notice. A salesperson who wants to sell an expensive laptop obviously has a reason to push it. A friend who always wants stability might discourage every risk, and that bias is harder to spot. When several people with different reasons give their opinions, the biases tend to cancel out. You can then see which advice is based on facts and which is based on how someone feels.

More opinions are not automatically better, though. You can collect so many that you cannot decide, or you can just pick the answer you already wanted. The goal is not to count votes. The goal is to understand why each person thinks what they think, and then weigh those reasons against what you care about. Say you are choosing a college. A parent might care most about prestige, a counselor might care about cost, and a recent graduate might wish they had not gone so far from home. The useful step is deciding which concern matters most for you, instead of letting the loudest person win.

Good advice-seeking also means picking people who know different things. Asking three people from the same background will probably get three similar answers and not much new information. It works better to ask one person who has done the thing before, one person who is an expert in the field, and one person who knows you well. Together they give a fuller picture.

In the end, the decision is still yours, and you are the one who has to live with it. Other people's opinions do not take that away. What they give you is a wider view of your options, a clearer sense of the trade-offs, and a better chance of noticing a detail you would have missed. That is why asking more than one person so often leads to a better choice.
