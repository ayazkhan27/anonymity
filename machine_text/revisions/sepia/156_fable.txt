Every June my cousin comes home with a packet in his backpack. It has a reading list of three novels, a worksheet for each one, and a rubric stapled to the back. Then every August, the night before school starts, he sits at the kitchen table and fills in the worksheets using chapter summaries he found online. He gets full credit for it. I don't think he has learned anything from those packets except how fast he can write when he's panicking. His school wants kids to keep learning over the summer, which is a good goal, but the packet is the wrong way to do it. I think students should design their own summer projects, and teachers should set the limits and check the plan.

The biggest reason is that people work harder on things they picked themselves. During the school year a teacher can push a student through an assignment he doesn't care about, because the teacher is right there five days a week. In the summer nobody is there. If a project is going to last through July, it's because the student is interested in it. Say a girl loves baking and decides to test how different amounts of baking soda change a cookie. She'll probably run that experiment six or seven times, because she gets to eat the results and because she really does want to know which batch comes out flat. She'll measure, write down what happened, and compare the batches. That's basically the scientific method and nobody had to force her to do it. If you gave that same girl a packet on the phases of the moon, she would do exactly what my cousin does.

Another reason is that designing a project is a skill, and students almost never get to practice it. When a teacher hands out a finished assignment, the hardest thinking is already done. Somebody already chose the question, decided what counts as an answer, and figured out how long each part should take. Then students get to college or a job and they're expected to know how to do all that. If a student plans to interview her grandfather about leaving Vietnam, she has to decide what to ask and how to record it. She also has to figure out what to do when he doesn't want to talk about a certain year. A student who wants to build a birdhouse has to price the wood, and then he finds out he measured wrong. Problems like that are frustrating, but I think they teach more than a rubric does.

Also, not everybody has the same summer, and a project designed by a teacher assumes they do. One student is at a lake for two months. Another one watches her little brothers every day while her parents are at work, and another has a job at a grocery store. If the project requires visiting a museum or having fast internet, that's easy for some students and almost impossible for others. When students design their own project, they can fit it to the summer they actually have. The girl who babysits could study how a four-year-old learns to read by teaching one. The boy at the grocery store could track prices for ten weeks and graph them.

The best argument against this is that some students will pick something too easy, or won't pick anything at all. That's a real problem, and it's why teachers shouldn't be left out. Before school lets out, every student should turn in a one-page proposal that says what the question is, what the plan is, and what the final product will be. The teacher either approves it or sends it back with comments. The teacher can also require every project to have some reading, some writing, and some kind of evidence. So the standards still come from the teacher, but the subject belongs to the student. And a teacher who reads thirty proposals in June gets thirty different projects in September instead of thirty copies of the same worksheet. That has to be more interesting for her too.

A summer project is supposed to keep students learning when nobody is making them. A packet can't do that. A question the student actually wants answered can.
