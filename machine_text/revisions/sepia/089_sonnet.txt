I agree with the principal's decision. Making every student join at least one extracurricular activity might sound like one more thing for busy people to do, but I think it would help students a lot more than they expect, and it would make our school better too.

The first reason is that a lot of kids never try anything new unless somebody pushes them. When I started middle school, I told myself I was not a club person. I went to class, came home, and did my homework. Then a teacher asked me to help the yearbook staff take pictures at a pep rally, just one time. I ended up staying the whole year. I learned how to crop a photo, how to meet a deadline, and how to argue nicely with an editor about which picture should get a full page. If nobody had pushed me I never would have found out I liked it. A requirement works the same way. It gives shy kids, or kids who are not sure, a reason to go through a door they would walk past.

The second reason is that activities teach things you can't learn in a classroom. In math I mostly work alone and my grade depends on me. On a soccer team, a debate squad, or student council, other people are depending on me. If the treasurer forgets to count the money from a bake sale, the whole dance budget is in trouble. Students learn to show up on time, finish what they said they would do, and work with people they didn't pick as friends. Colleges and jobs say they want these habits, and a requirement makes sure every student gets some practice.

Some people say students are already too busy, especially ones who have jobs or take care of their little brothers and sisters. That is a fair point, and the principal should deal with it. But the rule says at least one activity, not three, and the school can keep the choices wide and flexible. A student could join a club that meets once a week, help out in the school garden, or work on the stage crew for the spring play. A student with a part-time job could count a few hours of tutoring younger kids. One activity won't take over a schedule, and for most students it's a break from studying instead of extra work.

A third reason is that activities make our school feel more like a community. There are over a thousand students here, and it is easy to go four years only knowing the kids in your own classes. When students join the same band, service club, or track team, they meet people from other grades and other groups. I have seen eighth graders show sixth graders how to do a relay handoff, and I have seen friendships start between kids who would never have talked in the hallway. That makes the building friendlier, and kids who feel like they belong usually come to school more and care more about their grades.

In the end, school is not just about passing tests. It is about growing up into people who can do things and who want to learn. An extracurricular requirement is a small step toward that. It asks each student for a few hours a week and gives back confidence, teamwork, and friends. I would tell my classmates to support the principal's decision, and I think a lot of them will be glad they did once they find the activity they didn't know they would love.
