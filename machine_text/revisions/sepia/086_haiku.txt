Every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some people say a school cannot force a student to spend time outside of class on something he or she did not pick. I get that worry, but I agree with the principal. Required participation does not mean forced fun. It means every student gets a chance to find a place where they belong, and for a lot of students that place is not in a math book or a lecture hall.

The biggest benefit is that activities give students a reason to show up. A lot of kids who struggle in class can't see why school matters. When a student joins the track team, coming to school makes more sense. He has practice after lunch, teammates who expect him there, and a coach who notices when he misses. I have watched a girl who barely passed freshman English become the most reliable person on the yearbook staff. She had to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and fix page layouts at eleven at night. Her grades got better because she finally had a reason to care about what she was doing during the day.

Activities also teach skills that classrooms rarely cover. Student council asks students to plan events, raise money, and argue for their ideas in front of adults who may not agree. A student who runs a food drive learns how to budget, how to ask for donations without sounding desperate, and how to recover when a plan falls apart. None of this shows up on a standardized test, but employers and colleges care about it. Sports teach discipline and how to lose well. Debate teaches a student to listen to the other side before answering. Music ensembles show that one person's mistake affects everyone in the room.

Some critics say students who already have jobs, family responsibilities, or heavy class loads can't fit in another commitment. That concern deserves respect, and a good policy should account for it. But the requirement doesn't have to be rigid. A student can choose from dozens of options, including clubs that meet once a week or service projects that take a few hours a month. The school can also count volunteer work at a library or community center. The goal is not to fill every afternoon with practice. The goal is to make sure every student has at least one connection to the school beyond the bell schedule.

Opponents also say a student who isn't into sports or drama will be miserable in a required activity. But with so many options, it's unlikely that nobody finds anything interesting. A student who dislikes competition might enjoy photography, robotics, or chess club. A shy student might find that the gardening club gives him a quiet place to talk with other people. Plenty of students discover interests they never knew they had, only because someone asked them to try something new. Ask any adult with a lifelong hobby how it started, and many will say they tried it by accident.

Finally, schools are supposed to prepare students for life after graduation, and life is rarely a set of solo tasks. People work in teams, lead projects, and deal with conflict. Activities are a low-stakes place to practice those things. A student who spent a season on a team or a year on a committee walks into college or a first job with experience that a transcript alone can't show.

So I think the principal is right. Requiring at least one activity is not an attempt to control students' free time. It is a chance to find something that might change how a student sees school and himself. The policy won't work perfectly for everyone, but it gives every student a door to walk through, and that is worth the small effort it asks of each of them.
