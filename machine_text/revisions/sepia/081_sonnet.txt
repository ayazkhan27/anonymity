I agree with the principal that every student should have to do at least one extracurricular activity. Some kids are going to complain, and some will say they are already too busy after school. But school is supposed to get us ready for more than tests. A required activity gives every student a chance to learn stuff a regular class doesn't teach.

The main reason is that activities teach skills that grades don't show. Take yearbook. To finish one book, a group of students has to plan the pages, take pictures, sort them, write captions, and get everything to the printer on time. If one person falls behind, the whole book is late. Someone who does yearbook for a year learns how to manage their time. They learn to take advice from an editor, and they learn to count on other people and have people count on them. Student council is the same. If you help plan a fall dance, you have to make a budget, get teachers to help, and deal with people complaining that the music is too loud. Those are things jobs want you to be good at later, and in normal classes we barely get to practice them.

Another reason is that activities help students find where they fit in. Every school has quiet kids who come in, sit through class, and go home without really knowing anybody. I have seen this happen. A boy in my grade hardly said a word until he joined cross-country. He wasn't the fastest runner, but the team was nice to him on the long bus rides and at team dinners. By spring he was yelling for teammates he didn't even know the fall before. When you belong to something, you actually want to come to school. I have read that students who are in a club or on a team are less likely to drop out. If this rule helps even a few lonely kids find a group, it was worth it.

Some people say a requirement takes away choice. They think students should only join things they really care about, and that kids who are forced will be bored and not try. I get that worry, but there is still a lot of choice. The principal didn't say everybody has to play football. The list has sports, yearbook, and student council, and the school could add chess club, drama, band, a recycling committee, or robotics. If you don't like competition, you could paint sets for the spring play. If you love computers, you could run the school website. The rule just says you have to pick something. Picking from lots of options is still a real choice, and it pushes people to try things they would never think of. A lot of us don't know what we like until we try it.

Time is another worry. Some students have jobs, take care of younger brothers or sisters, or have a ton of homework. A good rule should think about that. The school can have activities that meet at lunch or only once a week, and it can let a job or family duties count in special cases. Being flexible makes the rule fair and still keeps the point. Even a busy student gets something from one small thing, because it gives them a routine with other people and something to look forward to.

Last, this habit matters after school too. Adults who volunteer, coach a kids' team, or sit on a neighborhood board usually started joining things when they were young. If you learn early that you can help a group and the group is better because of you, you will be ready to be a good citizen. A school that asks everyone to practice this is investing in the community they will lead someday.

So I support the principal's decision. Activities teach teamwork and responsibility, they help lonely students connect, and they let everyone find something they are good at. With good options and some flexibility, one activity per student is a small thing to ask for a big reward.
