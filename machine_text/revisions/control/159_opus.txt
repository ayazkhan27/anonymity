Every June, students leave school with the same packet. There's a reading log, some math worksheets, and maybe a book report with a rubric stapled on the front. By August most of those packets either get rushed in one crazy weekend or never get done at all. If the point of a summer project is to keep students learning, then schools should let students design their own projects, and teachers should act more like guides than the ones who make everything up.

The biggest reason is motivation. When a teacher designs the project, every student is supposed to care about the same thing at the same time, and that pretty much never happens. Take a kid who loves baseball. He might groan at a random essay assignment, but he would spend hours figuring out batting averages, tracking pitch speeds, and comparing players from different decades. That is actual statistics, and he would do it without anyone forcing him. When students pick the topic, the work stops feeling like a chore that follows them home and starts feeling like something that belongs to them. Stuff you learn because you are curious usually sticks a lot longer than stuff you learn just to check a box.

Second, designing a project is a skill that students need to practice. In school almost every decision is made for us, like what to read, when it's due, and how long it has to be. But adults have to set their own goals and plan how to reach them all the time. A summer project gives students a chance to practice this when it doesn't really count against them. For example, a girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden has to research what plants grow where she lives, budget for seeds and soil, stick to a watering schedule, and figure out what went wrong when her tomatoes split. No worksheet teaches you that kind of planning and problem solving.

Third, projects that students design can use the resources they actually have. Everyone's summer is different. One student spends July on their grandparents' farm, another works part time at a restaurant, and another babysits their little siblings every day. One assignment from a teacher can't fit all of those lives, but a project the student designs can. The restaurant worker could study how the kitchen keeps track of food costs, and the babysitter could write and draw a picture book for the kids she watches.

Some people argue that if teachers don't design the projects, students will pick ones that are way too easy or just give up on them. That is a fair point, but it is really an argument for having structure, not for teachers making every choice. Schools could have students turn in a short proposal before summer starts that explains what they will do, what they want to learn, and how they will show it. Teachers can approve it, suggest changes, and check in once or twice by email. At the end, students can present their projects to the class, which keeps them accountable and lets everyone learn from each other's summers.

Summer is one of the only times students get the freedom to follow their own interests. Schools should take advantage of that instead of filling it up with another packet. When students design their own projects, they learn the material, and they also learn how to teach themselves.
