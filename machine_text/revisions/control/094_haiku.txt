Every student should have to join at least one extracurricular activity. Some students will not like this rule. They think school is for classes and that a forced activity wastes time they could use for homework, a part-time job, or just resting. I understand why people feel that way, but I think the principal made the right choice. Being required to join something gives students a place to belong, teaches skills that classrooms do not really teach, and sometimes shows talents that would stay hidden otherwise.

The most important benefit is belonging. A lot of students come to high school and feel invisible. They sit in big classes, eat lunch fast, and go home without talking to anyone outside their small group of friends. An activity can change that. For example, think about a boy who joins the yearbook staff because it is the only elective left in his schedule. At first he only takes pictures of the cafeteria and the football field. Over the year he learns how to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and argue with editors about page layouts. By spring he knows dozens of people from different grades, and the teachers who advise the staff have become like mentors to him. If it was not required, he probably would never have walked into that darkroom at all.

Activities also teach skills that tests do not measure. A student on the debate team learns to research both sides of an argument and to speak when everyone is watching. A student on a soccer team learns that one missed pass affects the whole team, and that practicing on a cold Tuesday matters more than any single game. Student council members learn how to budget when they have to decide how to spend two thousand dollars from fundraising. They also learn to compromise when three classes disagree about a homecoming theme. People learn these lessons by doing things, and a requirement makes sure every student gets the chance to practice them.

Some critics say that forcing students to join turns activities into chores. This is true for some kids who get put in a club they hate. However, the rule does not make students pick a specific activity. It only says they have to try something. A student who does not like sports can choose chess, robotics, or the literary magazine. A student who is scared of public speaking can join the photography club or the school garden. Most people do not know what they enjoy until they try a few things, and the requirement makes sure that trying happens. Many adults can point to one hobby that changed the direction of their lives, and that hobby often started as something they only tried because they had to.

Finally, extracurricular activities connect what we learn in school to the real world. Classes can explain how the government works, but student council shows how a vote is counted and how a idea becomes a rule. Classes can talk about teamwork, but a band or a robotics team needs teamwork every single afternoon. These experiences help students get ready for jobs, colleges, and being citizens, where working together and taking initiative matter as much as grades.

Some people will say that one rule cannot fit every student. That is true, which is why the rule should be broad enough for each student to find something that fits. The principal's decision shows that school should do more than hand out information. It should help students become confident, connected, and curious people. For that reason, I agree that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity.
