Summer projects are supposed to help students keep what they learned during the school year. Over ten or twelve weeks off, a lot of it gets forgotten, and teachers call this the summer slide. So schools give out assignments to keep students thinking. But the real question is who should decide what the project is. I think summer projects should be designed by the student, with a frame from the teacher. When students pick the question they want to answer, they will remember more, and it will actually be learning and not just busywork.

The biggest reason is motivation. In summer there is no bell and no teacher standing at the front of the room. If a student does an assignment then, it is because they decided to. A packet of forty math problems asks a fifteen year old to give up beach days for something that feels exactly like a Tuesday worksheet. Most students do what you would expect. They rush it the night before school starts or copy the answers from a friend. A project the student came up with is different. A guy who loves basketball and wants to find out how shooting angles change accuracy will measure and record and redo the math without anybody nagging him, because he wants to know the answer. Effort that comes from curiosity lasts longer than effort that comes from a deadline.

Student-designed projects also teach skills that teacher-designed ones don't. Picking a topic, deciding what counts as evidence, making a schedule, and finishing without anyone watching are things adults have to do all the time. When the teacher designs the project, the hardest part is already done, which is deciding what to do. The student just follows directions. If a student has to plan it herself, she goes through the whole process of learning. For example, a girl who is bothered by the litter in her neighborhood park could survey the trash for six weeks, sort it by type, and write a proposal asking the city for more recycling bins. That uses science, math, writing, and civics all at once, and no worksheet could really script that. Because she built it, she understands why each step matters.

Some people worry that students who design their own work will pick something easy, or nothing at all. Someone might say they will "watch documentaries" and call it research. That is a fair concern, but it is a reason for guidance, not a reason to take away control. The best way is a partnership. Before school ends, the teacher sets the requirements: the project needs a question, a method, a record of progress, and a final product shared in the fall. Students turn in a short proposal, and the teacher either approves it or sends it back to be fixed. This keeps the standards high while the student still picks the subject. A teacher can tell a vague idea from a workable one, and a quick talk in June can change "watch documentaries" into "compare how three documentaries show the same historical event and review which one is most accurate."

Another objection is fairness. Some students have books, computers, and parents who help them, and others don't. A teacher-designed project at least gives everybody the same task. But equal tasks don't give equal results, because the student with more resources still finishes faster and does better. Student design can actually make the gap smaller, since a kid can build a project out of what she has. A student with no internet can interview her grandmother about family history, write a short biography, and draw a map of where the family has lived. That only takes a notebook and a conversation. Teachers can help even more by giving a list of free options and letting the fall presentation be an essay, a model, a video, or a talk. I think that is the fairest way to do it too.

Finally, student projects make the first week back in September better for everyone. Instead of collecting a pile of packets that all look the same, a teacher gets a room full of different discoveries. Students have something real to share and classmates learn from each other. Hearing a classmate explain what she found about the park litter is a lot more interesting than reading a stack of answers to the same twenty questions. In the end, a project is only useful if the student actually does it, and students do the things they care about.

Summer should keep students learning, but it should also let them enjoy it. Teachers know what a good project needs, and students know what they are interested in. If teachers set the standards and students pick the topics, schools can get both rigor and excitement. That is what turns a required assignment into work students might do even if nobody asked them to. I think that is the best way to do summer work.
