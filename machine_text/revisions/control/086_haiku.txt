Every student should have to take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some people say that the school cannot make a student spend time outside of class on something he or she did not pick. I get that concern, but I agree with the principal's decision. Having to join something does not mean forcing fun. It means every student gets a chance to find a place where they belong, and for a lot of students that place is not in a math book or a lecture hall.

The biggest benefit is that activities give students a reason to come to school. A lot of kids who struggle in class have a hard time seeing why school matters. When a student joins the track team, coming to school makes more sense. He has practice after lunch, teammates who expect him there, and a coach who notices when he misses. I once saw a student who barely passed freshman English become the most reliable person on the yearbook staff. She learned how to interview classmates, meet deadlines, and fix page layouts. Her grades got better because she finally had a reason to care about what she did during the day.

Activities also teach skills that classrooms usually do not cover. Student council, for example, asks students to plan events, raise money, and argue for their ideas in front of adults who might not agree. A student who runs a food drive learns how to make a budget, how to ask for donations without sounding desperate, and how to bounce back when a plan falls apart. These things do not show up on a standardized test, but employers and colleges care about them. Sports teach discipline and how to lose with respect. Debate teaches a student to listen to the other side before answering. Band and choir teach that one person's mistake affects everyone in the room.

Some critics say that students with jobs, family responsibilities, or heavy homework loads cannot fit in one more thing. That concern is fair, and a good policy should take it into account. However, the rule does not have to be strict. A student can choose from many options, including clubs that meet once a week or service projects that only take a few hours a month. The school can also count volunteer work at a library or community center. The goal is not to fill every afternoon with practice. The goal is to make sure every student has at least one connection to the school outside of the bell schedule.

Other people say a student who is not into sports or drama will be miserable with a required activity. But there are so many choices that it is unlikely nobody will find something they like. A student who does not like competition might enjoy photography, robotics, or chess club. A shy student might find that the gardening club gives him a quiet spot to talk with other people. Lots of students find interests they never knew they had, just because someone asked them to try something new. Ask any adult with a lifelong hobby how it started, and many of them will say they tried it by accident.

Finally, schools are supposed to get students ready for life after graduation, and real life is rarely done alone. People work in teams, lead projects, and deal with conflict. Extracurricular activities are a low-pressure place to practice those skills. A student who has spent a season on a team or a year on a committee walks into college or a first job with experience that a transcript cannot show.

For these reasons, I believe the principal is right. Requiring one activity is not a way to control students' free time. It is an invitation to discover something that might change how a student sees school and himself. The policy will not work perfectly for everyone, but it gives every student a door to walk through, and that is worth the small effort it asks of each of them.
