I agree with the principal's decision that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some students will complain at first, and a few will say they are already too busy. But school is supposed to get us ready for more than tests, and a required activity gives everyone a chance to learn things that a classroom does not teach.

The main reason is that these activities teach skills that grades can't measure. Take yearbook for example. To finish one book, a team of students has to plan the pages, take pictures, write captions, and turn it in before the printer's deadline. If one person falls behind, the whole book is late. A student on that team learns how to manage time, how to listen to an editor, and how to count on other people and have them count on you. Student council is the same way. A council member who helps plan a fall dance has to make a budget, ask teachers for help, and deal with complaints when the music is too loud. These are the same skills that employers say they want, and most students don't get much practice with them during a normal school day.

Another reason is that extracurriculars help students find where they belong. Every school has quiet kids who come in, sit through class, and leave without really knowing anyone. A boy in my grade hardly talked until he joined the cross-country team. He was not the fastest runner, but the team welcomed him on the long bus rides and at team dinners, and by spring he was cheering for teammates he had never met the fall before. Belonging to something makes a student want to come to school. Research on attendance and dropout rates often finds that students who are part of a club or team are less likely to quit school early. If the rule gets even a few lonely students to find a group, then it was worth it.

Some people say a requirement takes away choice. They think students should only join what they really care about, and that forced members will be bored and careless. That is a fair point, but this decision still leaves a lot of choice. The principal did not say everyone has to play football. The list includes sports, yearbook, and student council, and the school can add chess club, drama, band, a recycling committee, or a robotics team. A student who does not like competition can paint sets for the spring play. A student who loves computers can run the school website. The rule says everyone has to pick something. Picking from many options is still a real choice, and it pushes students to try things they would never have thought about. A lot of us don't know what we like until we try it.

Another worry is time. Some students have jobs, take care of younger siblings, or have a lot of homework. A good policy should think about that. The school can offer activities that meet at lunch or only once a week, and it can let a part-time job or family duties count in special cases. Being flexible makes the rule fair without ruining the point of it. A busy student gets something from one small commitment, because it gives them a routine with other people and something to look forward to.

Finally, this habit matters after high school too. Adults who volunteer, coach a kids' team, or serve on a neighborhood board usually started by joining things when they were young. Learning early that you can help a group, and that the group is better because of you, prepares students to be good citizens. A school that asks every student to practice that is investing in the community they will lead someday.

For these reasons, I support the principal's decision. Extracurricular activities teach teamwork and responsibility, they help lonely students connect, and they give everyone a chance to find a talent. With good options and some flexibility, requiring one activity for each student is a small thing to ask for a big reward.
