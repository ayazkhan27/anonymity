Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June my cousin came home with the same packet. It was like forty pages of reading passages and math review, stapled in the corner, and it was due the first day of school. And every August he did the whole thing in the last four days at the kitchen table while complaining the entire time. He always finished it, but I really doubt he could tell you one thing that was in it by October. That packet is a big reason why I think summer projects should be designed by students. Teachers should set the rules for the project, but they shouldn't pick what it's about.

The biggest reason is pretty simple. Summer work only gets done if the student actually decides to do it. During the school year a teacher can check on you every day and answer questions and notice if you're falling behind. In July nobody is checking anything. So if a student picked the project themselves, there's a way better chance they keep working on it for ten weeks with no one watching. For example, a girl who loves to cook could spend her summer testing what happens to cookies when you change how much baking soda goes in. She could write down the results each time and then write about what she found out. That's measurement, ratios, and the scientific method, and she's doing it because she wants to know the answer. If you gave that same girl a worksheet on ratios, she would do it the night before it was due.

Another reason is that designing your own project teaches you how to plan, which a teacher-made project can't really do. Normally the assignment sheet tells us the topic, what the final product should look like, and all the steps. We almost never have to figure that out on our own. But colleges and jobs expect people to handle tasks where nobody tells you every step. A summer project is a safe place to practice that because not much can go wrong. Say a student decides to build a working app and has zero coding experience. They are probably going to find out it's too much and have to cut it down to a simple quiz game. That's a real lesson about not taking on too much, and all it cost was a couple frustrating weeks.

There is also a fairness problem that I think people forget about. Not everyone has the same kind of summer. Some kids travel, some work thirty hours a week, some babysit their little brothers and sisters all day, and some don't have good internet at home. When a teacher makes one project for everybody, it's basically assuming everybody has the same summer. But a student who works at their family's restaurant could make a project about it, like keeping track of which hours are the busiest and then coming up with a better schedule for the workers. That fits into the life they already have. A required museum visit or an online research project might not fit at all.

Some people would say that students will just pick something easy, or they won't learn what the school needs them to learn. I think that is a fair point, and it's why teachers still need to be involved. A teacher could make everyone turn in a written proposal in May. They could also list the skills the project has to show, like using at least three sources or writing a certain amount, and give out the rubric ahead of time. So if someone proposes to "watch movies all summer," it gets sent back. But if someone proposes comparing three movies to the books they are based on, that gets approved. The teacher protects the standards and the student comes up with the idea.

In conclusion, teachers know what students need to learn, but students know what they will actually do in the middle of July. A summer project only works if both of those things are true. Letting students design the project inside a teacher's rules is the best way to make that happen.
