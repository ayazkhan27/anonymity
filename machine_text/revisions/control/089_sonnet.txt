I agree with the principal's decision. Making every student join at least one extracurricular activity might sound like extra work for people who are already busy, but I think it would make our school better and help students more than they think.

The first reason is that a lot of students never try new things unless somebody pushes them. When I started middle school, I said I was not a club person. I went to class, went home, and did my homework. Then my teacher asked me to help the yearbook staff take pictures at a pep rally, one time. I ended up staying the whole year. I learned how to crop a photo, how to meet a deadline, and how to argue with an editor about which picture should get a whole page. If nobody had pushed me, I never would have found out I liked it. A requirement works the same way. It gives shy kids or kids who are unsure a reason to go through a door they would walk past.

The second reason is that activities teach you things you can't learn in a classroom. In math class I mostly work alone and my grade depends on me. On a soccer team or in student council, other people depend on me. If the treasurer forgets to count the money from a bake sale, the whole dance budget is in trouble. Students learn to show up on time, finish what they said they would do, and work with people who are not their friends. Colleges and jobs want people with these habits, and a requirement makes sure every student gets practice.

Some people say students are already too busy, especially kids who have jobs or take care of their little brothers and sisters. That is a fair point, and the principal should think about it. But the rule says at least one activity, not three, and the school can give lots of choices. A student could join a club that meets once a week, help in the school garden, or work on the stage crew for the spring play. A student with a job could count a few hours of tutoring younger kids. One activity will not take over someone's schedule, and for most students it is a break from studying instead of another thing to stress about.

A third reason is that activities make our school feel more like a community. Our school has over a thousand students, and it is easy to go a whole year only knowing the kids in your classes. When students join the same band, service club, or track team, they meet people from other grades and other groups. I have seen eighth graders get help from older kids on how to do a relay handoff, and I have seen friendships start between people who would never have talked in the hallway. These connections make the school friendlier, and students who feel like they belong usually come to school more and care more about their grades.

In conclusion, school is not just about passing tests. It is about growing up to be a smart and curious person. An extracurricular requirement is a small step toward that. It only asks for a few hours a week, and it gives back confidence, teamwork, and friends. I think everyone should support the principal's decision, and I bet a lot of students will be glad when they find an activity they did not know they would love.
