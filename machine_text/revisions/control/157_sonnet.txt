Summer Projects Should Be Student-Designed

Every June, students walk out of school with a long stretch of free time and, in a lot of districts, a packet of assignments to keep their brains from turning to mush. Studies on the summer slide show that students can lose a good amount of their math and reading skills over the break. But who designs the work matters a lot. Summer projects should be student-designed, with teachers acting as guides, because when students own the work they put in more effort, learn for real, and build skills that a worksheet never could.

The biggest reason for this is motivation. A project made by a teacher shows up in the middle of summer as one more thing to do, and students treat it like that. They put it off until August, copy answers from a friend, or rush it the night before school starts. When students pick their own topics, it flips. A student who loves basketball could spend the summer looking at shooting percentages and making a model that predicts which players will improve the most. If that same student got a generic packet of probability problems, they would probably do the bare minimum. Interest doesn't run out, and student-designed projects use it.

Another reason is that designing a project is an education by itself. Teacher-made assignments hide the hardest part of real work, which is deciding what to do. Someone already picked the question, the format, and the deadline. A student who has to write a proposal has to take a vague interest and turn it into a real question, guess how long each part will take, and plan for the problems that will come up. These are the habits that college professors and employers say young people are missing. A girl who decides to grow a small vegetable garden and write down how it grows has to budget, research soil, record observations, and change her plan when the tomatoes die. She learns science, but she also learns to manage her own time, and no packet teaches that.

Critics say students left alone will pick easy projects or nothing at all. This is a fair worry, but it is a reason for structure, not for teacher control. Schools can make students turn in a short proposal in June that the teacher looks over and approves, with a few check-ins during the summer and a presentation in the fall. The teacher sets the standard, like requiring research, a written reflection, and proof of steady effort, while the student picks the subject. A teacher can also push a weak idea into something harder. A student who wants to "watch documentaries" could be asked to compare how three films show the same historical event and write about the differences.

Another objection is fairness. Teacher-designed projects give every student the same assignment, which makes grading easy and equal. But being the same is not the same as being fair. Students come back from summer with very different resources. One might have a library card and a quiet house, while another works a part-time job and watches younger siblings. A strict assignment that assumes museums, travel, or a computer helps the students who already have advantages. A flexible project lets each person make something that fits their life. The working student could interview coworkers about their jobs and write an essay about the local economy, which only takes a notebook.

Finally, think about what students actually remember. Hardly anyone remembers a worksheet from the summer after sixth grade. A lot of people remember the time they built a birdhouse, wrote a short story, or taught themselves to code a simple game. Those things stick because the person made them. A student who finishes a project they chose comes back to school with something to show and the confidence of having shaped it.

Summer learning matters, and schools are right to protect it. The best way to do that is to trust students to design the work, hold them accountable with proposals and presentations, and let teachers coach instead of command. When learning belongs to the student, it lasts way past September.
