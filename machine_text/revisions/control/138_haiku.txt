When people need advice, they should talk to more than one person. I believe this because one adviser can only see things from one point of view. That person's experience, values, and even blind spots change every answer they give. If you ask several people, you get a wider view and can see the choice more clearly before you make it.

Imagine someone who is thinking about taking a job offer in a different city. A close friend might focus on feelings and say that moving means leaving behind the people who support them. A coworker who moved to a new city before might talk about the practical problems, like how expensive housing is, how much paperwork a move takes, and how long it took to feel at home. A mentor in the same field might talk about career growth and whether the new job teaches skills that will still matter in five years. Each person notices something the others missed. The person deciding ends up knowing much more than any one adviser could have told them.

Multiple opinions also show disagreement. When advisers agree, it is a good sign, because it means the advice is probably based on something real and not just one person's opinion. When they disagree, that is useful too. It shows the person that the choice has real trade-offs. It also makes them ask why each adviser thinks differently. Often the reasons show assumptions the person never thought about. For example, the friend may care most about being stable, while the mentor cares most about growing. Noticing that difference can help the person figure out what they really want.

Hearing several viewpoints also protects people from one loud voice. People often trust the first confident answer they hear, especially if it comes from someone they look up to. A strong adviser can push a decision toward what they want without meaning to. When a person compares different perspectives, they stay in charge. They become the one who weighs the facts instead of just picking the closest opinion.

Of course, there are limits. Having too many advisers can be confusing, and gathering opinions can become a way to avoid deciding at all. The goal is not to ask everyone who will listen. It is better to pick a small group with different strengths: someone who knows the person well, someone who knows the topic, and someone who will be honest about risks. Even after getting advice, the person still has to make the final choice, and nobody else can take that responsibility.

In the end, asking more than one person for advice is a smart habit. It helps people avoid thinking too narrowly, shows hidden trade-offs, and leads to a decision that considers many things instead of just one view. A person who listens to several thoughtful people and then honestly weighs what they heard is much more likely to make a choice they can stand behind.
