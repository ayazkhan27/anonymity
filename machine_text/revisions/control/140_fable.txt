Every June, a lot of schools send students home with a summer project along with their report cards. The idea is to keep us learning over the ten weeks of break, and I think that goal makes sense. Studies on the "summer slide" say students can lose a month or more of reading and math over the summer. The real question is who should get to decide what the project is. In my opinion, summer projects should be designed by students, but teachers should set a few basic requirements and approve the plan before school gets out.

The biggest reason is motivation. During the school year a teacher can remind you, check in, and push you to keep going. In July there is nobody doing that. The only thing that is going to make a student do the project well, and not just throw it together the night before school starts, is actually caring about it. When a teacher designs the project, everybody gets the same thing, so it doesn't really fit anyone. One summer I was assigned a packet of thirty reading response worksheets. I did all of them in two days at the end of August, basically copying the same format from page to page. A week later I couldn't have told you what any of the books were about. On the other hand, a friend of mine got to pick her own topic for a science class. She spent the summer testing which homemade fertilizer grew the tallest tomato plants in her backyard. She measured them every three days and came back in September with a whole notebook of data and a strong opinion about compost. Nobody had to nag her because the project was hers.

Student-designed projects also make more sense because not everyone has the same summer. One student might work thirty hours a week at a grocery store. Another might be staying with grandparents in a different state, and another might have to watch younger siblings every day. One assignment written in May can't work for all of those situations. But if students design their own project, they can build it around what their summer is really going to be like. The student with the grocery store job could track how prices change over the summer and try to explain why. The one staying with grandparents could interview them about what their town was like fifty years ago and write a family history. Those projects still involve reading, writing, math, and research, and a teacher never could have come up with them because the teacher doesn't know what each student's summer looks like.

Another benefit is that designing a project is a skill by itself. You have to come up with a question, figure out how to answer it, guess how long each step will take, and change your plan when something goes wrong. Those are the things colleges and employers always say young people aren't good at. When the teacher designs the project, the teacher does all of that thinking and the student just does the work. When the student has to plan it, the planning is part of what they learn.

Some people would say that students will just pick something easy if it's left up to them, and honestly some would. Somebody might say they are going to watch a TV series and write a paragraph about it. Another argument is that teachers know what skills we need for the next grade and students don't. Both of these are fair points, but I think they show that students need guidance, not that the design should be taken away from them completely. The teacher could require every project to have a certain amount of reading, a written piece of a certain length, and some connection to next year's classes. Students would turn in a one-page proposal in May, and the teacher would send the weak ones back to be fixed. This way the teacher still makes sure the quality is there, but the project still belongs to the student.

In conclusion, summer projects exist because schools want learning to keep going when the building is closed. But learning that happens with nobody watching has to come from the student. A project that a student chose, planned, and fit around their own summer is much more likely to be done carefully and remembered later than a packet that was handed to the whole class. Schools should set the standards and then let students design the work.
