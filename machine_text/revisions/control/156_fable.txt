Every June my cousin comes home with a packet in his backpack. It has a list of three novels, a worksheet for each one, and a rubric stapled on the back. And every August, the night before school starts, he sits at the kitchen table and fills in the worksheets using chapter summaries he found online. He gets full credit for it. He didn't learn anything, except maybe how fast he can write when he's panicking. I think his school has a good goal but they are going about it the wrong way. Summer projects should be designed by the students, with teachers setting some limits and approving the plan.

The biggest reason is that people work harder on things they picked themselves. During the school year a teacher can push you through an assignment you don't care about, because they see you five days a week. In the summer nobody is there. The only thing that is going to keep a project going in the middle of July is if the student is actually interested in it. For example, say a girl loves baking and she decides to test what happens to cookies when you change the amount of baking soda. She's probably going to do that six or seven times, because she gets to eat them and because she really wants to know which batch comes out flat. She'll measure things, write down the results, and compare them. That's basically the scientific method and nobody had to force her. But if you give that same girl a packet about the phases of the moon, she'll do exactly what my cousin does.

Another reason is that designing a project is a skill by itself. When a teacher hands you an assignment that's already finished, the hardest thinking has been done for you. Somebody else picked the question, decided what a good answer looks like, and figured out how long it should take. Students almost never get to practice that, and then we get to college or a job and we're just supposed to know how. If a student wants to interview her grandfather about leaving Vietnam, she has to decide what to ask him, how to record it, and what to do if there's a year he doesn't want to talk about. If a student wants to build a birdhouse, he has to find out how much the wood costs, and then probably find out he measured wrong. Those problems are frustrating, but I think you learn more from them than from a rubric.

Also, not everybody has the same summer. A project made by the teacher kind of assumes they do. One kid is at a lake for two months. Another one is watching her little brothers all day while her parents are at work. Somebody else has a job at a grocery store. If the project says you have to visit a museum or have fast internet, that's easy for some students and almost impossible for others. When students design their own project, they can make it fit the life they really have. The girl who is babysitting could teach her four-year-old brother to read and study how he learns. The boy at the grocery store could keep track of prices for ten weeks and make a graph.

Some people would say that if students get to choose, some of them will pick something way too easy or just not do anything. That's true, and it's the reason I don't think teachers should be left out. Before school ends, every student should hand in a one-page proposal that says their question, their plan, and what they will turn in at the end. The teacher can approve it or give it back with comments. The teacher could also make a rule that every project needs some reading, some writing, and some kind of evidence. That way the standards still come from the teacher, but the topic belongs to the student. Plus, a teacher who reads thirty proposals in June gets to read thirty different projects in September instead of the same worksheet thirty times, which has to be less boring for her too.

The whole point of a summer project is to keep students learning when nobody is making them. A packet can't do that. A question that a student actually wants to answer can.
