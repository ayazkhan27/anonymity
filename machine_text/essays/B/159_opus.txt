Every June, right when school lets out, a lot of students get handed a packet of summer work. Usually it's a reading list and some worksheets that the teacher picked out. Most kids shove it in a drawer and then panic about it the last week of August. I think that is a sign the system isn't working. Summer projects should be student-designed, with some basic guidelines from teachers, because students learn more when they care about what they are doing, they build skills they actually need, and the work is more likely to get done.

The first reason is motivation. When a teacher assigns the same project to a hundred students, it is impossible for it to interest all of them. Last summer my English class had to read a novel about a whaling ship and answer twenty questions about it. I did it, but honestly I skimmed most of it and looked up the answers. My friend Marcus on the other hand spent the same summer teaching himself to fix up an old bike and writing a blog about each step. He did way more reading and writing than I did, and he did it because he wanted to. If he had been allowed to turn that in as his summer project, it would have counted for something.

Second, designing your own project teaches skills that a worksheet never will. In the real world nobody hands you a packet with instructions. You have to figure out a goal, make a plan, manage your time and fix problems when things go wrong. A student-designed project forces you to practice all of that. For example, if someone decides to research the history of their town, they have to figure out where to find sources, maybe interview older neighbors, and organize everything into a final product. That is basically what college and jobs expect of you, so why not start now?

Third, students are more likely to actually finish a project they chose. Teacher-designed projects often feel like punishment, so kids put them off or copy from each other. When it's your own idea, you have a reason to keep going. Also, it's harder to copy someone else's work when everyone is doing something different.

Some people would argue that if students design their own projects, they will just pick something easy or do nothing at all. That is a fair concern. Some students would try to get away with "I watched YouTube videos." But this can be fixed by having teachers set requirements. For instance, the project could need a proposal approved before school ends, a certain number of hours, and a final presentation or paper in the fall. That way teachers still keep standards high, but students get to choose the topic.

In conclusion, summer projects should be designed by students. When students choose, they stay motivated, learn real life skills, and actually complete the work. Teachers should still guide the process, but the ideas should come from the students themselves. Summer is supposed to be a break, and learning about something you love doesn't feel like work.
