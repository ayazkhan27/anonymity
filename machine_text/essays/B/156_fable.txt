Every June, right before the last bell, a teacher hands out a packet. Everybody groans, shoves it in the bottom of their backpack, and forgets about it until the last week of August. I have done this pretty much every year since middle school. If schools are going to require summer projects so that we keep learning over break, then the projects should be designed by the students, not the teachers. Students work harder on something they picked, they learn skills that a packet can't teach, and a student-designed project actually fits the kind of summer each kid is having.

The first reason is simple. People put more effort into things they care about. Last summer my English class had to read an assigned novel and fill out a chart with twenty quotes and what they meant. I read the book in four days at the end of August and I honestly could not tell you the main character's last name now. That same summer, though, I spent weeks helping my uncle rebuild the engine on an old pickup truck, and I can still explain how a carburetor works. Nobody graded me on the truck. I learned it because I wanted to know. If I had been allowed to turn that into my project, maybe by writing up what we did or making a video about it, I would have done better work and the school would have gotten what it wanted, which is a student who kept his brain on during the summer.

Second, designing your own project is a skill by itself. When a teacher designs the project, all the hard thinking is already done. The teacher picked the topic, the steps, and the due date, and the student just follows directions. But after high school nobody hands you a packet. In college you have to pick your own research topic, and at a job your boss might just say "figure out why sales are down" and walk away. A student who has to come up with an idea, plan it out over ten weeks, and finish it is practicing time management and problem solving. My older sister had to do a senior capstone where she chose her own topic, and she said the hardest part was not the work, it was deciding what to do and sticking with it. That is exactly the kind of practice we should be getting earlier.

Third, summers are not the same for everybody. Some kids go on trips, some work forty hours a week at a fast food place, and some babysit their little brothers and sisters all day while their parents are working. A teacher-designed project assumes everyone has the same time and the same stuff at home, like a computer or a ride to the library. A student-designed project can bend around real life. A kid who works at a restaurant could track how the business handles its busy hours. A kid watching siblings could teach them to read and keep a journal on their progress. Those are real projects, and they don't punish a student for having responsibilities.

Some people will say that if students design the projects, they will pick the easiest thing possible, or the project won't connect to anything in school. That is a fair worry. I know some people in my grade who would try to count playing video games as a project. But this is easy to fix. Teachers can require a short proposal in May and approve it, and they can give a rubric that says the project needs research, some kind of writing, and a final product. That way the teacher still sets the standard, but the student owns the idea.

Summer projects are supposed to keep us learning, not just keep us busy. A packet that gets rushed in three days at the end of August does not do that. Letting students design their own projects, with a teacher checking the plan, would get more effort, teach more useful skills, and be fair to everyone. Schools should give us the chance to prove it.
