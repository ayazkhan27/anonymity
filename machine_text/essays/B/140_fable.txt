Every June, my school hands out a packet. It's usually stapled, about fifteen pages, and it has the same questions for every single kid in the grade. I can tell you honestly that most people I know do that packet the last week of August, some of them the night before school starts. If the whole point of a summer project is to keep students learning over the break, then that packet is not doing its job. I believe summer projects should be student-designed, because students work harder on things they picked, they actually remember what they learned, and designing a project is a skill by itself.

The first reason is motivation. When a teacher designs the project, it feels like homework that followed you home for the summer. When you design it, it feels like yours. Last summer my cousin, who goes to a different school, got to choose his own project for science. He likes fishing, so he tested the water in three different ponds near his house and kept track of what he caught in each one. He was out there almost every week, and nobody had to remind him. Meanwhile I was reading a book I didn't choose and filling out a chart about the characters. I finished it, but I skimmed the last hundred pages. We both technically completed a summer project, but only one of us was learning the whole summer.

Second, students remember more when the project connects to something they care about. I can't tell you much about that book now, and it's only been a year. My cousin can still explain what pH is and why one pond had more algae than the others. This makes sense if you think about it. People remember the things they do way better then the things they are told to memorize. A student who loves cars could research how engines work and rebuild a lawnmower motor. A student who wants to be a nurse could interview people who work at a hospital. Those students are going to walk into school in September with real knowledge, not a packet they copied off a friend.

That leads to another point, which is cheating. When every student has the exact same assignment, it is very easy to copy. Everybody knows this happens. Answers get sent around in group chats by July. If each student designs their own project, there is nothing to copy because nobody else is doing the same thing. So student-designed projects are actually more honest, even though some people would expect the opposite.

Third, designing the project teaches skills that school doesn't always teach. You have to come up with an idea, make a plan, figure out how long it will take, and then follow through without someone checking on you every day. That is basically what college and jobs are like. In two years I will be picking my own classes and managing my own time. It would be better to practice that now on a summer project then to try it for the first time when it really counts.

Some people will say that if students design their own projects, they will pick something too easy, or it won't have anything to do with school. That is a fair concern. Some kids would probably try to turn in a project about playing video games for eight weeks. But this problem has a simple solution. Teachers can require students to turn in a proposal before summer starts, and the teacher can approve it or ask for changes. The teacher could also give a rubric that says the project needs research, some kind of writing, and a final product. This way the teacher still sets the standards, but the student chooses the topic. Teachers also know what we need for next year, so they can give a list of skills the project should include.

In conclusion, summer projects should be designed by students, with teachers guiding them. Students will put in more effort, remember more, cheat less, and learn how to manage their own work. A packet teaches you how to finish a packet. A project you built yourself teaches you something you will actually keep.
