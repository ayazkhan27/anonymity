Every June, right before school lets out, teachers hand out a packet. Everybody knows what happens to that packet. It goes in the bottom of a backpack and stays there until the last week of August, when it gets done in two nights at the kitchen table. Some schools require summer projects so students keep learning over break, and I think that is a fair goal. But if the school really wants us to learn something, the projects should be designed by the students and not by the teachers.

The first reason is that students actually care about things they pick themselves. Last summer I had to read a book from a list and fill out a chart about the characters and the themes. I didn't like any of the books on the list, so I picked the shortest one. I honestly can't tell you much about it now. That same summer, though, my cousin and I spent weeks trying to fix up an old dirt bike that was sitting in my uncle's garage. We watched videos, we looked up how a carburetor works, and we had to figure out how much money we could spend on parts. Nobody assigned that to me, and I learned way more from it than from the chart. If my project could have been the dirt bike, I would have had something real to turn in and I would have worked harder on it.

Another reason is that everybody's summer is different. A teacher designing one project for a hundred and fifty students can't know what each kid's summer looks like. Some students have jobs. Some watch their little brothers and sisters all day while their parents work. Some go out of the country to visit family for a month and don't have internet. A project that says "visit a museum and write a report" is easy for one kid and almost impossible for another. If students design their own, they can build it around what they are already doing. A student who works at a restaurant could do a project about how the kitchen keeps food safe, and a student visiting family in another country could interview their grandparents about what school was like there.

Designing the project is also part of the learning. In a few years we are going to be in college or at a job where nobody hands us a packet with every step written out. We will have to come up with a plan, set our own deadlines, and decide if what we made is good enough. Summer is a good time to practice that because the stakes are low.

Some people would say that if students make their own projects, they will just pick something easy, like "I watched a show and wrote a paragraph about it." That could definitely happen. Teachers also know what we need for next year better than we do. But this problem can be fixed without teachers designing the whole thing. Students could turn in a short proposal in May, and the teacher could approve it or tell them to make it harder. The teacher could also give a few rules, like it has to include some writing and some research. That way the teacher still checks the quality but the idea belongs to the student.

Summer projects are supposed to keep us learning, not just keep us busy. A packet that gets rushed in August doesn't do that. A project that a student chose and planned has a much better chance of being something we remember when school starts again.
