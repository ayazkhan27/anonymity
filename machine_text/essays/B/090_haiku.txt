Should Every Student Join an Extracurricular Activity?

Our principal has decided that every student must take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some people think this is a good idea, and some people think it is unfair. I agree with the decision because activities help students learn things they cannot learn in regular classes, they help students make friends, and they give students a reason to stay excited about school.

First, extracurricular activities teach skills that are hard to learn from a textbook. For example, my cousin did not like school very much until she joined the volleyball team. At first she was nervous and missed a lot of serves, but her coach taught her to keep practicing and to stay positive after a mistake. Now she is one of the best players on the team, and she says she learned how to work hard when something is difficult. Students who join a club or a sport can learn teamwork, time management, and how to handle losing, and these skills matter in every job and every part of life.

Second, activities help students meet new people. In a big school, it is easy to spend the whole day with the same small group of friends and never talk to anyone else. When a student joins the yearbook club, for example, they might work with kids from different grades who like art or writing. My friend Marcus was very shy when he started sixth grade. He did not know anyone in the student council. After a few months of meetings, he felt comfortable enough to give a speech in front of the whole school. Activities give shy students a safe place to find friends who share their interests.

Third, some students do not know what they are good at until they try something new. If students are required to join an activity, they will have chances to discover talents they never knew they had. A student who thinks they hate music might join the school band and discover they love playing the drums. A student who never thought about helping people might join the student council and find out they enjoy planning events for the school. Without a requirement, many students would never take that first step.

Some people argue that students who already have a lot of homework or who have jobs at home should not be forced to join an activity. I understand this concern, and I think it is important to listen to students. However, the principal can make the rule flexible. Students can choose from many different options, including short clubs that meet only once a week, so no one has to give up their responsibilities. The rule is not about adding stress. It is about making sure every student has something to look forward to after the last bell.

In conclusion, I agree with the principal's decision. Extracurricular activities teach valuable skills, help students make friends, and let students discover new talents. A school should want every student to feel like they belong somewhere, and an activity is one of the best ways to make that happen.
