Every June, right when school lets out, a lot of students get handed a packet of summer work. Most of the time it ends up shoved in a backpack until the last week of August, when it gets rushed through the night before school starts. Schools want students to keep learning over the break, which makes sense, but the way they go about it usually doesn't work. I believe summer projects should be student-designed, because students learn more when they actually care about what they are doing, and because it teaches them skills they will need later in life.

The first reason is motivation. When a teacher assigns a worksheet packet or a book report on a book nobody picked, students treat it like a chore. But when students get to choose, they put real effort in. For example, last summer my friend Marcus was supposed to read a novel and write a summary for English. He did the bare minimum. At the same time, on his own, he spent weeks teaching himself how to edit videos and made a short documentary about his grandfather's time in the Navy. He interviewed him, researched the ship he served on, and wrote a script. That project had more reading, writing, and research than the assignment ever did, and he actually enjoyed it.

The second reason is that designing a project teaches responsibility and planning. In the real world, nobody hands you a packet with step-by-step instructions. Adults at jobs have to figure out a goal, make a plan, and manage their own time. If a student has to come up with their own project, like building a garden and tracking how different fertilizers affect plant growth, they have to set deadlines and solve problems on their own. Those are skills that a fill-in-the-blank worksheet can't teach.

Some people might argue that if students design their own projects, they will just pick something easy or do nothing at all. That is a fair concern. However, teachers can still be involved without designing the whole thing. Schools could require students to submit a short proposal before summer and have a teacher approve it, and then present the finished project in the fall. That way there is still accountability.

In conclusion, summer projects should be designed by students. When students choose, they are more motivated and they learn how to manage their own work. With a little guidance from teachers, summer projects could be something students look forward to instead of something they dread.
