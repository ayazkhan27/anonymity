Every June, teachers hand out a packet right before the last bell, and every August most of us are finishing it the night before school starts. I think that tells you a lot about how well teacher-designed summer projects work. If schools are going to require summer projects, the students should be the ones who design them.

The biggest reason is that people work harder on things they actually care about. Last summer my English class had to read a book off a list and fill out a chart about themes and symbols. I picked the shortest book and did the chart in two days. I honestly don't remember much of it. But that same summer my cousin and I spent weeks trying to fix up an old dirt bike in his garage. We watched videos, read the manual, and had to figure out why the engine kept flooding. Nobody assigned that, and I learned more about how engines work than I did from any worksheet. If that could have counted as my project, I would have written about it gladly.

Another reason is that students have different summers. Some kids have jobs, some watch their little brothers and sisters all day, and some travel. A teacher can't plan one project that fits everybody. A student who works at a restaurant could do a project on how the business handles money, and a student visiting family in another country could study the history of that place. When students design it, the project fits their real life instead of fighting against it.

Some people would say that students will just pick something easy if nobody is telling them what to do. That is a fair point, and some probably would. But teachers could fix this by approving the idea before summer starts and giving a simple rubric. The student still does the designing, the teacher just makes sure it is real work.

Summer projects are supposed to keep us learning, not just keep us busy. Students learn best when they have a say in what they are doing. Schools should trust us enough to let us design our own projects.
