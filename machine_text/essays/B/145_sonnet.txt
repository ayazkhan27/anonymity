Summer projects are supposed to keep students learning when school is out, but the real question is who should design them. I think students should design their own summer projects, with a teacher approving the plan. When students choose what they work on, they put in more effort, they learn skills that last, and they actually finish what they start.

The first reason is motivation. Most students already feel tired of school by June. If a teacher hands out a packet of worksheets or a required book report, it feels like homework that followed you home. A student who picks a topic they care about is a different story. For example, my cousin loves cars, so for a project she rebuilt a small lawnmower engine over the summer. She read manuals, watched tutorials, and kept a journal of every problem she ran into. She spent more hours on that than she ever would have on a worksheet, and nobody had to nag her. Interest is what makes a person keep going when nobody is watching, and summer is a time when nobody is watching.

The second reason is that designing a project is a skill in itself. A student who plans their own project has to decide what the goal is, break it into steps, set deadlines, and figure out what to do when something goes wrong. These are the same things adults do at jobs and in college. When a teacher designs everything, the student only follows directions, and following directions is easier but it teaches less. In college, nobody is going to tell me exactly what to do each week, so I would rather practice managing myself now. A student-designed project is practice for that, and a teacher-designed one is more of the same thing we do all year.

Some people argue that teachers should design the projects because they know what the curriculum needs. This is a fair point. A teacher knows which skills students will need in the fall, and some students might choose something too easy or too random. Without any guidance, a kid could say their project is playing video games for three months. But this problem can be fixed without taking the choice away. The teacher can ask each student to turn in a proposal before the school year ends. The proposal would explain the goal, the steps, and how the student will show what they learned. The teacher could then approve it or ask for changes. That way the teacher still protects the standards, but the idea belongs to the student.

Another worry is that students will not do the work if nobody makes them. However, I think that is more likely to happen with teacher-designed projects. If a student does not care about an assignment, they will wait until the last week of August and rush through it, or copy from a friend. Students who picked their own topic have a reason to care, because the project is about something they wanted to know. Even a small project, like learning to cook ten meals from different countries or writing a short story collection, can show real learning when a student chose it.

In conclusion, summer projects should be student-designed with teacher approval. This gives students motivation, teaches them to plan and manage their own work, and still keeps the learning on track. If schools want students to keep learning over the summer, they should let students learn something they actually want to learn.
