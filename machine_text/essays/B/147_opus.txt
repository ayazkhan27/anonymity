Every June, right when school lets out, a lot of students get handed a packet of summer work. Most of the time it gets shoved in a drawer until the last week of August, when it gets rushed through in one night. Schools assign summer projects so students keep learning over the break, which is a good goal. But if the point is actually to keep students learning, then summer projects should be designed by the students themselves, with some basic guidelines from teachers.

The first reason is motivation. When a teacher picks the project, it usually feels like more homework, and students do the bare minimum just to get it done. When students get to choose, they pick something they actually care about. For example, my friend Marcus is obsessed with cars. If his summer project was a worksheet on the causes of World War One, he would put it off until the last day. But if he could do a project on how engines work, or the history of the Ford Motor Company, he would probably spend hours on it without anyone forcing him to. Learning sticks better when you are interested in it, and that is the whole reason for a summer project in the first place.

Second, student-designed projects teach skills that teacher-designed ones do not. Planning your own project means you have to come up with a question, figure out where to find information, and manage your time without someone checking in every day. These are the skills that colleges and jobs actually expect from us. Last summer I designed my own project where I tracked the birds that came to a feeder in my backyard and made graphs of which species showed up at different times of day. Nobody told me how to set it up, so I had to figure out how to organize my data and what counted as a good observation. I learned more about science from that than from most of the labs I did in class, because I was the one making the decisions.

Some people argue that if students design their own projects, they will just pick something easy or not do anything at all. That is a fair concern. Not every student is going to be responsible on their own, and teachers need to make sure the work is actually challenging. But this problem can be solved without taking away student choice. Teachers could require students to submit a short proposal before summer starts, explaining what they will do and what they hope to learn. The teacher could approve it or ask for changes. Then at the end of summer the student could present what they did to the class. This way students still have freedom, but there is still accountability.

In the end, summer projects only work if students actually learn something from them. A teacher-designed packet might look more organized, but it mostly teaches students how to fill in blanks quickly. A student-designed project, with a teacher approving the plan, gives students a reason to care and teaches them how to learn on their own. That is the kind of learning that will continue long after summer is over.
