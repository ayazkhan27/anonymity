Every June, students across the country walk out of school with the same feeling: finally, a break. But many schools do not let that break be completely free. They assign summer projects so that students keep learning while school is out. The big question is who should design these projects, the teachers or the students. I believe summer projects should be student-designed, because students work harder on things they care about, they learn important life skills, and they are less likely to just copy someone else's work.

The first reason is motivation. Summer is the one time of year when students can choose how to spend their days. If a teacher hands out a packet or a list of required readings, it feels like school never ended. A lot of students will put it off until the last week of August and then rush through it just to turn it in. When a student picks their own project, it is different. For example, a student who loves cars could design a project about how electric engines work compared to gas engines. They could visit a repair shop, watch videos, and write up what they found. That student is going to spend hours on it without anyone nagging them, because it is something they actually want to know. I have seen this in my own life. I once spent a whole weekend learning how to edit videos just because I wanted to make a highlight reel of my brother's soccer games, and I remember more from that than from most worksheets.

The second reason is that designing a project teaches skills that a teacher-designed project cannot. When a teacher makes the plan, the student only has to follow directions. But when a student makes the plan, they have to decide on a goal, figure out the steps, and make a schedule to finish before school starts. These are the same skills people use in college and at a job. A student who plans a summer project to build a small vegetable garden, for example, has to research what grows well in their climate, budget money for seeds and soil, and keep track of the plants every week. If something goes wrong, like the tomatoes dying in a heat wave, they have to figure out why and fix it. That kind of problem solving is real learning. Teachers can explain time management in class all year, but students only really learn it by doing it themselves.

The third reason is that student-designed projects are more original. When every student in a class gets the same assignment, it is easy to copy from friends or look up answers online. Everyone knows that the night before school starts, group chats fill up with people asking for the answers to the summer packet. If each student has their own project, there is nothing to copy. A teacher can tell right away if someone really did the work, since each project is personal and different. This also makes the teacher's job more interesting, because instead of grading thirty of the same essay, they get to see thirty different ideas.

Of course, some people disagree. They say that teacher-designed projects are better because teachers know what students need to learn. A teacher might want to make sure everyone practices math skills or reads certain books so they are ready for the next grade. This is a fair point, and I do think students might pick projects that are too easy if nobody checks on them. Some students might also not know what to choose and end up doing nothing. However, there is a simple fix for this. Teachers can still be part of the process by setting some basic rules and approving each project idea before summer starts. For instance, a teacher could say that the project must include writing, research, and a final presentation, but let the student choose the topic. That way the teacher makes sure the project is challenging, while the student still gets to make it their own.

In conclusion, summer projects should be designed by students, with guidance from teachers. Students are more motivated when they care about the topic, they learn planning and problem solving skills, and they cannot copy from each other when every project is different. Summer is supposed to be a time to relax, but it can also be a time to explore something interesting. If schools trust students to design their own projects, they might be surprised by how much students learn when they do not feel like they are being forced to.
