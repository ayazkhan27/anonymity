Every June, right when school lets out, a lot of students get handed a packet or a list of instructions for a summer project. Most of the time it gets shoved in a backpack and forgotten until the second week of August. I think the reason for that is simple. The projects are designed by teachers, and students don't feel any connection to them. Summer projects should be student-designed, because students learn more when they care about what they are doing, they build skills like planning and responsibility, and they get a chance to explore things that school doesn't have time for.

First, students put in real effort when the project is something they actually chose. Last summer my school assigned a reading log where we had to read two books off a list and answer ten questions about each one. I did it, but honestly I rushed through the questions the night before school started. Meanwhile, I spent hours that same summer teaching myself how to fix up an old bike I found in my uncle's garage. I watched videos, I bought parts, and I messed up the brakes twice before I got them right. Nobody graded that, but I learned way more from it than from the reading log. If my school had let me design a project around that bike, like writing about the mechanics or keeping a journal of the repairs, I would have turned in something I was proud of.

Second, designing a project teaches skills that following directions doesn't. When a teacher hands you a project, they already made all the decisions. They picked the topic, the length, the deadline, and how it will be graded. All the student has to do is fill in the blanks. But in college and at a job, nobody is going to tell you exactly what to do step by step. You have to figure out a goal, make a plan, and manage your time. A student-designed project forces you to practice that. You have to come up with an idea, decide how to show what you learned, and keep yourself on track for two months without anyone reminding you.

Some people might argue that if students design their own projects, they will just pick something easy or not do anything at all. That is a fair concern. But the answer isn't to take away the choice completely. Teachers could still set some basic requirements, like the project has to take a certain number of hours or include a written reflection at the end. Students could also turn in a short proposal before summer starts so the teacher can approve it. That way there is still accountability, but the student is in charge of the actual idea.

Finally, summer is the one time students have to explore interests that don't fit into a normal class schedule. Someone might want to learn to code, start a garden, or interview their grandparents about family history. These are real learning experiences, and a teacher-designed project would probably never include them.

In conclusion, summer projects should be designed by students. When students choose, they care more, they learn to plan, and they discover what they are really interested in. That is the whole point of learning over the summer anyway.
