I agree with the principal's decision. Requiring every student to join at least one extracurricular activity might sound like one more demand on busy people, but I think it would make our school better and help students more than most of them expect.

The first reason is that many students never try anything new unless someone nudges them. When I started high school, I told myself I was "not a club person." I went to class, came home, and did homework. Then a teacher asked me to help the yearbook staff take photos at a pep rally, just once. I ended up staying for the whole year. I learned how to crop a photo, meet a deadline, and argue politely with an editor about which picture deserved a full page. If nobody had pushed me, I would never have discovered that I liked it. A requirement works the same way. It gives shy or unsure students a reason to walk through a door they would otherwise walk past.

The second reason is that activities teach skills a classroom cannot. In math class, I work mostly alone, and my grade depends on me. On a soccer team, a debate squad, or the student council, other people depend on me. If the treasurer of the student council forgets to count the money from a bake sale, the whole dance budget is in trouble. Students learn to show up on time, to finish what they promised, and to work with people they did not choose as friends. Colleges and employers say they want these habits, and a requirement guarantees every student gets practice at them.

Some people argue that students are already too busy, especially those who have jobs or take care of younger brothers and sisters. That is a fair concern, and the principal should deal with it directly. The rule says "at least one" activity, not three, and the school can keep the choices wide and flexible. A student could join a club that meets once a week, volunteer for the school garden, or help set up the stage crew for the spring play. A student with a part-time job could count a few hours of tutoring younger kids. One activity does not take over a schedule, and for most students it is a break from studying, not an extra burden.

A third reason is that activities make a school feel like a community. Our school has more than a thousand students, and it is easy to spend four years knowing only the kids in your own classes. When students join the same band, the same service club, or the same track team, they meet people from other grades and other groups. I have seen seniors help freshmen learn how to run a relay handoff, and I have seen friendships form between students who would never have spoken in the hallway. Those connections make the building friendlier and make students more likely to feel they belong, and students who feel they belong tend to attend more often and care more about their grades.

In the end, the purpose of school is not only to pass tests but to grow into capable, curious people. An extracurricular requirement is a small step in that direction. It asks each student for a few hours a week and gives back confidence, teamwork, and friends. I would urge my classmates to support the principal's decision, and I think many of them will be glad they did once they have found the activity they did not know they would love.
