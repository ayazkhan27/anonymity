Phones and the Driver's Seat

Almost everyone I know carries a phone, and almost everyone I know has looked at it while driving. I have done it too, at a red light, telling myself that the car was not moving so it did not count. The question of whether drivers should be allowed to use cell phones in any capacity is usually asked as if the answer must be a flat yes or a flat no. I think the honest answer is narrower than either. Drivers should not be allowed to hold, read, or type on a phone while the vehicle is in motion or stopped in traffic, but a phone that is mounted and running navigation, or connected for an emergency call, is a different thing and should stay legal.

The case against handheld use is not complicated. Driving safely requires eyes on the road, hands on the wheel, and attention on what is happening ahead. Texting takes away all three at once. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has said that reading a text takes a driver's eyes off the road for about five seconds, and at highway speed that is roughly the length of a football field covered without looking. Nobody would agree to drive that distance with their eyes closed, yet that is what a quick glance at a message amounts to. The same agency attributes thousands of deaths every year in the United States to distracted driving, and those numbers are probably low, since a driver who caused a crash has little reason to admit a phone was involved.

What makes phone use worse than other distractions is that the person paying the price is often someone else. A driver who eats a sandwich or fiddles with the radio is also distracted, but a phone pulls attention in a deeper way, because a conversation or a message asks for a reply. The pedestrian in the crosswalk, the cyclist on the shoulder, and the family in the next lane never agreed to that risk. Laws exist for exactly this situation, where one person's convenience is paid for with another person's safety. We accept speed limits and drunk driving laws on the same reasoning, and very few people now argue that those are unfair limits on freedom.

Some people say hands-free calling solves the problem. It solves part of it. A driver using a speaker or a headset keeps both hands on the wheel and both eyes forward, which is clearly better than holding the phone. But studies of driver attention have found that the mind stays partly in the conversation, and reaction times get slower even when the hands are free. A passenger will stop talking when traffic gets difficult, because the passenger can see the road. The person on the other end of a call cannot. For that reason I would not ban hands-free calls outright, since such a law would be nearly impossible to enforce, but I would not pretend they are harmless either. Drivers should keep them short, and new drivers, who are still learning to manage the car itself, should not be allowed to make them at all.

There are uses of a phone that make driving safer, and a total ban would throw those out. Navigation is the obvious one. A phone in a dashboard mount giving spoken directions is far less dangerous than a lost driver slowing down to read street signs or unfolding a paper map. Being able to call for help after a crash, or to report a drunk driver or a hazard on the road, is another. A law that forbids phones "in any capacity" would make criminals out of people doing these things, and a law that sensible people ignore weakens respect for the sensible parts of it.

So the rule I would defend is simple enough to remember. Set the route before pulling out, put the phone in a mount, and do not touch it again until the car is parked. If something truly cannot wait, pull over. The message will still be there in ten minutes, and so will everyone on the road.
