Drivers should not be allowed to use cell phones in any capacity while operating a vehicle. The convenience of a phone is real, but it does not outweigh the risk of a crash that can kill someone.

Driving demands a person's full attention. Many people believe that hands-free calling is a safe compromise, but research on distraction suggests otherwise. Distraction comes in three forms: visual, manual, and cognitive. A hands-free call removes the manual part, yet the driver's mind is still pulled toward the conversation. Drivers on a call miss exits, fail to notice pedestrians, and react more slowly to brake lights. Looking at the road is not the same as seeing it. Because the mental distraction remains, a law that permits hands-free use only gives drivers a false sense of safety.

Texting is even worse. Reading a message at highway speed means traveling the length of a football field with eyes off the road. Nothing that arrives on a screen is urgent enough to justify that. Most messages can wait ten minutes, and the ones that cannot wait, such as an emergency, can be handled by pulling over first.

Critics argue that a total ban is too strict, since phones also provide navigation and music. This objection has a simple answer. A driver can set a route and a playlist before leaving the driveway, and many cars now have built-in systems that are designed for use on the road. A phone mounted for directions and left alone is different from a phone that is actively used. A clear rule that bans handling the device and using it for calls or messages is also easier to enforce than a patchwork of exceptions, because an officer can see a phone in use without having to guess what the driver is doing with it.

Society already accepts limits on driving behavior, from speed limits to drunk driving laws, because the road is shared and one person's choices endanger many others. Phone use belongs in the same category. A call or a text is never worth a life, so drivers should wait until the car is parked.
