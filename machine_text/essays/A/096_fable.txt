One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When I first heard that our principal wanted every student to join at least one extracurricular activity, my reaction was the same as most of my friends: who is he to decide what I do after three o'clock? After thinking about it for a few days, though, I changed my mind. I agree with the decision, as long as the school keeps the list of choices wide.

The main reason is that most of us do not try things unless something pushes us. In sixth grade my mother made me sign up for the school newspaper because she was tired of watching me play video games until dinner. I complained for two weeks. By November I was interviewing the cafeteria manager about why the pizza had changed, and I found out I liked asking adults questions they did not expect. I would never have walked into that room on my own. A requirement does for every student what my mother did for me. It gets you through the door, and after that you can decide for yourself whether you like it.

Activities also teach things that classes do not. In math, if I do not do my homework, only my grade suffers. On the soccer team, if I skip practice, ten other people have to run a play with a hole in it. Yearbook staff have a printer deadline that does not move for anybody, and student council members have to stand up and defend an idea in front of people who think it is dumb. Those are the situations adults deal with at work every day, and a worksheet cannot copy them.

There is a social reason too. Our school has about nine hundred students, and it is easy to spend three years knowing only the twenty people in your classes. Clubs mix the grades and the friend groups. A quiet seventh grader in the chess club ends up sitting across from an eighth grader who would never have spoken to him in the hallway. For a new student, or one who eats lunch alone, having a place to be on Tuesday afternoons where people expect him can change the whole year.

The strongest argument against the rule is that some students really have no time. Some watch younger brothers and sisters until their parents get home, some have a long bus ride, and some already do dance or church groups outside of school. That objection is fair, and the principal should answer it. Outside activities ought to count, and there should be clubs that meet during lunch or only once a month. One activity is a small requirement. Nobody is being asked to play three sports. A student could satisfy it by helping in the library for half an hour a week.

Some people also say that forcing students makes them resent the activity. Maybe, for a few. But we are forced to take science and gym, and plenty of people end up liking those. I would rather have a handful of students mildly annoyed than have hundreds leave middle school never having found out what they are good at.

So I support the principal's decision. He should give us plenty of options and make room for students with real conflicts, and then he should hold us to it.
