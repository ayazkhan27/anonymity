Learning From Home Should Be a Choice

When my cousin broke her leg in the fall of her sophomore year, she missed almost five weeks of school. Her teachers sent packets home, and a friend dropped off notes when she remembered to, but by the time my cousin could manage the stairs at her high school again she was so far behind in chemistry that she never really caught up. She passed, barely, and she still says that class is the reason she decided she was "bad at science." I think about her whenever people argue about distance learning, because a camera in the back of that chemistry room would have changed her whole year. Students would benefit from being able to attend classes from home, as long as it is offered as an option and not forced on everyone.

The most obvious benefit is for students who cannot physically get to school. Injuries and long illnesses are the clearest cases, but there are plenty of others. Some students live an hour or more from the nearest high school and spend two hours a day on a bus. Some have a parent who works nights and need to watch younger siblings on certain mornings. Some have anxiety or a chronic condition that makes a full day in a crowded building very hard, even when they are perfectly capable of doing the work. Under the traditional system, every one of these students is simply marked absent, and absence is one of the strongest predictors of failing a class. An online option turns a lost day into a regular day. The student hears the same lesson, asks questions and turns in the same assignment as everyone else.

Distance learning also gives students access to classes their own school cannot offer. A small rural school might have twelve students who want to take calculus and nobody qualified to teach it. A student in a city school might want to study Japanese or computer science and find that neither is on the schedule. With video conferencing, one good teacher can reach students in several buildings at once. I took an online statistics course last year for exactly this reason, since my school only offered it during the same period as band. The course was harder than I expected, and I had to learn to email the teacher when I was confused instead of waiting for her to notice my face. Still, I got to take a class I would otherwise have gone without, and that seems like a real gain.

That experience points to another benefit, which is that learning from home teaches students to manage themselves. Nobody rings a bell in your kitchen. You have to log in on time, keep track of deadlines and decide for yourself when to stop scrolling and start the reading. These are the habits college and most jobs demand, and many students arrive at college without them. It is better to practice them at sixteen, with parents and teachers still checking in, than to discover at eighteen that nobody is going to remind you about anything.

The strongest objection is that students at home will not actually learn. They will leave the camera off, play games in another window and miss the friendships and group work that make school more than a set of lectures. This worry is fair, and many students did fall behind when schools closed completely and everyone was online at once. But that was an emergency in which nobody had a choice. An option is different. A student who learns better in a classroom can stay there, and most probably will. Schools can also set conditions, such as requiring a certain grade average to stay in the online program or asking remote students to come in for labs and tests. The fact that some students would misuse the option is not a good reason to deny it to the ones who need it.

School buildings are not going away, and they should not. For most students on most days, a classroom full of other people is still the best place to learn. But the students who cannot be there, or who want a class their school does not have, deserve more than a packet of worksheets. If my cousin had been able to watch her chemistry class from the couch, I think she would tell a different story about herself today.
