When my cousin was deciding whether to buy a used car from a neighbor, he asked three people what they thought. His father told him the price was fair. A friend who works at a repair shop told him to look under the hood first. His older sister asked him whether he could still afford insurance after paying for it. None of the three gave him the whole answer, but together they gave him something close to it. That is the main reason asking more than one person for advice leads to better choices: each person sees only part of the problem.

Everyone gives advice from their own experience, and experience is limited. The father in that example had bought many cars, so he knew prices. He did not know much about engines. The friend knew engines but had no idea what my cousin earned at his job. If my cousin had stopped after the first conversation, he would have bought the car that afternoon. Because he kept asking, he found out that the timing belt needed replacing, and he used that to bring the price down by four hundred dollars. One opinion would have cost him money.

A second reason is that people have their own biases, and those biases are hard to notice when you hear from only one source. Suppose a student is choosing between two high schools and asks only her best friend. The friend will probably recommend the school she is attending herself, partly because she likes it and partly because she wants company. That advice is honest, but it is tilted. If the student also talks to a teacher, a parent, and someone who goes to the other school, the tilt becomes visible. She can see where the friend's opinion comes from and weigh it accordingly. Hearing several voices lets a person separate what is true about the choice from what is true about the adviser.

Multiple opinions also help when they agree. If I ask four people whether I should quit the soccer team to focus on my grades, and all four say the same thing for different reasons, I can feel more certain than if I had heard it once. Doctors work this way. A patient who is told she needs surgery will often get a second opinion, and when the second doctor reaches the same conclusion independently, the patient goes into the operation with more confidence. When the doctors disagree, that matters too, because it tells her the question is harder than it first appeared and deserves more thought.

Some people argue that too many opinions only cause confusion. That can happen, especially if someone is hoping another person will make the decision for them. But the solution is not to ask fewer people. It is to remember that advice is information and the choice still belongs to the person who has to live with it. My cousin did not do what any one adviser said. He checked the engine, negotiated the price, worked out the insurance, and then bought the car.

Asking several people takes more time than asking one, and it sometimes produces answers we would rather not hear. Still, a decision built on several points of view rests on more knowledge, fewer blind spots, and a clearer sense of how sure we ought to be. That is worth a few extra conversations.
