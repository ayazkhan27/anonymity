Every June, students leave school with a stack of summer assignments and a long stretch of free time. Schools hand out these projects so that learning does not stall for ten weeks. That goal makes sense. What makes less sense is assuming that a teacher, writing one assignment for a hundred students, is the best person to design the work. Summer projects should be designed by students, with teachers setting a few clear guidelines. A project a student chooses gets finished with more effort, teaches skills that a classroom rarely reaches, and fits the different lives students lead over the break.

The first reason is motivation. In summer there is no bell and no teacher in the room, so the work only gets done if the student wants to do it. A teacher-designed project, such as reading an assigned novel and answering twenty questions, often waits until the last weekend of August and then gets rushed. A project the student picked works differently. A student who loves baseball and decides to track the batting averages of her favorite team, then graph how they change across the season, will check the numbers every morning because she cares about the result. She is practicing statistics without anyone forcing her, and that kind of steady effort is exactly what schools say they want from summer work.

The second reason is that designing a project is itself a skill worth learning. During the school year, teachers decide almost everything: the topic, the deadline, the format, and the grading rubric. Students rarely get practice at choosing a question, planning the steps to answer it, and managing their own time. Those are the skills adults use at work and in college. A student who decides to build a small garden and record how different amounts of water affect tomato plants has to form a question, set up a fair test, keep a log, and adjust when a heat wave wipes out half the seedlings. No worksheet could teach that much about planning and persistence.

The third reason is that students spend their summers in very different places. One student works at a grocery store, another babysits younger siblings, and another travels to visit grandparents in another country. A single teacher-designed assignment cannot fit all of those situations. A student-designed project can. The student who works at the grocery store could study how prices change over the summer. The student visiting family abroad could interview relatives about their history and write a short family biography. Each project matches the life the student is actually living.

Critics argue that students left on their own will pick projects that are too easy. That risk is real, but it is easy to manage. Teachers can require a short proposal before school ends, approve it, and set expectations, such as a written reflection or a presentation in September. The teacher still guides the work without controlling every detail.

Summer projects exist to keep students learning. Students learn the most when the work belongs to them, so they should be the ones to design it.
