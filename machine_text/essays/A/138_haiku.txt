When people ask for advice, they often talk to more than one person, and there is good reason for it. A single adviser, however wise or well-meaning, sees the situation through one lens. Their experience, their values, and even their blind spots shape every answer they give. Gathering several opinions widens that lens and makes it easier to see the choice clearly before committing to it.

Consider someone deciding whether to accept a job offer in a different city. A close friend might focus on the emotional side and point out that the decision will mean leaving a support network behind. A former coworker who has relocated before might explain the practical hurdles, such as housing costs, the paperwork involved in moving, and how long it took them to settle in. A mentor in the industry might speak to career trajectory and whether the new role offers skills that will matter in five years. Each person highlights something the others missed, and the decision-maker ends up with a far more complete picture than any one of them could have provided.

Multiple opinions also help by exposing disagreement. When advisers agree, that agreement is informative, because it suggests the advice rests on something solid rather than on one person's preference. When they disagree, the disagreement itself is useful. It tells the person that the choice involves real trade-offs, and it prompts them to ask why each adviser reached a different conclusion. Often the reasons reveal assumptions that the person had not examined. The friend may value stability while the mentor values growth, and recognizing that difference can clarify what the person actually wants.

Seeking several viewpoints also protects against the pull of a single persuasive voice. People tend to trust the first confident answer they hear, especially when it comes from someone they respect. A dominant adviser can unintentionally steer a decision toward their own priorities. By comparing several perspectives, the person keeps more control over the outcome. They become the one who weighs the evidence rather than the one who simply accepts the closest opinion.

There are limits, of course. Too many advisers can create confusion, and collecting opinions can become a way of avoiding the decision altogether. The goal is not to poll everyone who will listen but to choose a small group with different strengths: someone who knows the person well, someone with relevant expertise, and someone who is willing to speak candidly about risks. Once the advice is gathered, the person still has to decide, and that responsibility cannot be handed off to others.

In the end, asking more than one person for advice is a practical habit. It guards against narrow thinking, reveals hidden trade-offs, and encourages a decision that reflects many considerations rather than a single viewpoint. A person who listens to several thoughtful voices, and then weighs them honestly, is far more likely to make a choice they can stand behind.
