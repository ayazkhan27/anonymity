One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When our principal announced that every student would have to join at least one extracurricular activity, the complaints started before the announcement was over. People said it was unfair, that school already takes up enough of the day, that nobody should be forced into a club. I understand the reaction, and I had some of it myself. But after thinking it through, I agree with the decision. One activity is a small requirement, and the students who would never sign up on their own are the ones who stand to gain the most from it.

The strongest reason is that most of us do not know what we are good at until somebody makes us try. I joined the school newspaper in ninth grade only because a teacher put my name on the list without asking. I was annoyed for about two weeks. Then I was given a story about the cafeteria switching food suppliers, and I had to interview the kitchen manager, a man I had walked past every day without once speaking to. I found out I liked asking questions and that I could put a story together on a deadline. None of that would have happened if the choice had been left to me, because I would have gone home and watched videos. A lot of students are in the same position. They are not lazy or uninterested. They have simply never been pushed through the door, and a requirement does the pushing.

Activities also teach things that classes do not. In a classroom, the work is mostly individual. You take your own test and get your own grade. On a team or a committee, your work depends on other people and theirs depends on you. A yearbook does not get finished if the photographer skips the game she was supposed to cover. A soccer team loses if the defenders do not talk to each other. Student council members have to sit in a room and argue about how to spend a small budget, and then live with a decision that half of them voted against. These are the situations adults deal with at work every day, and school gives us very few chances to practice them. Requiring one activity guarantees that every student gets at least some of that practice before graduating.

There is a social argument too. A school with a thousand students can be a lonely place. It is easy to go from class to class, eat lunch with the same two people or with nobody, and leave at the last bell without anyone noticing. An activity puts a student in a smaller group that meets regularly and has a reason to be together. A boy I know transferred here in the middle of tenth grade and barely spoke for a month. He joined the robotics club because his counselor insisted, and by spring he was the one explaining the wiring to new members. He did not need a lecture about making friends. He needed a room to be in and a job to do.

The most serious objection is about time. Some students work after school, some take care of younger brothers and sisters, and some have long bus rides home. This is a real problem, and the principal's rule will only be fair if the school plans for it. But the answer is to make the requirement flexible, not to drop it. Some clubs can meet during lunch or in an advisory period. An activity that meets once a week should count the same as one that meets every day. A student with a job could be allowed to count that job, since it teaches responsibility just as well as a club does. With those adjustments, the rule asks for perhaps an hour or two a week, which nearly everyone can manage.

Another objection is that forcing people takes the fun out of it, and that a club full of students who do not want to be there will be worse for everyone. I think this underestimates how wide the choices are. The requirement is not that everyone must play football. A student can pick chess, drama, the environmental club, the literary magazine, choir, coding, or volunteer tutoring. If nothing fits, students should be allowed to start a new group with a teacher's sponsorship. When the menu is that long, the student who claims to hate every item on it has usually not looked at it very carefully.

School is supposed to prepare us for more than tests. It should send us out knowing something about ourselves, able to work with other people, and connected to at least a few classmates. Classes alone do not reliably do that. One required activity, chosen by the student and scheduled with some common sense, can. I think the principal made the right call, and I expect most of the students complaining this week will agree by the end of the year.
