Every June, my cousin used to come home with a stapled packet of reading questions for a novel he had not chosen, and every August he filled it in during the last three days of vacation. He could not tell you the main character's name by October. That packet is what a teacher-designed summer project often turns into, and it is the reason I think students should design their own.

The purpose of a summer project is to keep students learning while nobody is watching them. A teacher can force work during the school year because there are deadlines every week and a person standing at the front of the room. In July there is neither. The only thing that gets a fifteen-year-old to sit down and work on a Tuesday afternoon in summer is some real interest in the thing itself. A student who picks her own topic has that interest from the start. One who is handed a topic has to be lucky.

Consider what a student might actually choose. Someone who spends the summer working at an uncle's auto shop could document how a brake system works and explain the physics of friction and heat behind it. A student who babysits might track how a toddler's vocabulary grows over ten weeks. A kid obsessed with basketball could collect shooting statistics and learn what a percentage really tells you and what it hides. None of these would appear on a teacher's assignment sheet, yet each one involves research, writing, and careful thinking, and each fits the life the student is already living over the break.

Designing the project is also part of the learning. Deciding on a question, figuring out how big it should be, and planning how to finish by September are skills that college and jobs demand and that school rarely lets students practice. A teacher who designs everything removes the hardest and most useful step.

The fair objection is that some students will choose something lazy, or will not know where to begin. That is a real risk, and the answer is for teachers to approve a short proposal in the spring and to set basic requirements, such as a written piece and a list of sources. The teacher sets the standard, and the student supplies the subject.

Summer belongs to students in a way the school year does not. A project they built themselves is one they are far more likely to finish honestly, and to remember.
