When schools first moved classes online during the pandemic, many people predicted that distance learning would become the future of education. A few years later, most students are back in classrooms, and the reasons for that return say a lot. I believe that students can benefit from having the option to attend classes from home, but only as an option, used in specific situations, and not as a replacement for being in school.

The strongest argument for distance learning is access. Some students cannot get to school reliably. A student with a chronic illness, such as severe asthma or an immune disorder, might miss weeks of class every year. Without an online option, that student falls behind and has to scramble to catch up through worksheets and borrowed notes. With video conferencing, the same student can hear the lecture, ask questions, and stay connected to the class. The same is true for students who live in rural areas far from a school that offers advanced courses. A small town high school may not have enough students to justify a calculus or Mandarin teacher, but an online class can bring that course to a student who would otherwise never get the chance.

Distance learning can also help students who learn better at their own pace. Recorded lessons can be paused and replayed. A student who is confused by a chemistry demonstration can watch it three times at home without feeling embarrassed in front of classmates. For self-motivated students, this flexibility can lead to deeper understanding.

However, the experience of the last few years showed that learning from home has serious drawbacks for most students. Many students struggled to stay focused when the classroom was a laptop on a kitchen table, competing with phones, siblings, and the temptation to turn the camera off and drift away. Teachers reported that it was much harder to tell who was lost, because a muted square on a screen does not show the puzzled look a teacher would notice in person. Test scores in many districts dropped, and the students who lost the most ground were often the ones who already had the fewest resources at home, such as a quiet room or a fast internet connection.

School is also about more than academic content. Students learn how to work in groups, resolve disagreements, and build friendships by being around one another. Lab experiments, sports, band practice, and simply talking with friends at lunch are hard or impossible to reproduce over video. Many students during remote learning described feeling lonely and unmotivated, and that isolation affected their mental health as well as their grades.

For these reasons, the best approach is a balanced one. Schools should offer distance learning to students who genuinely need it, such as those who are sick, live far away, or want a course their school cannot provide. At the same time, in-person school should remain the default, because for most students the classroom provides structure, support, and human connection that a screen cannot replace. Used wisely, distance learning can widen opportunities without taking away what makes school valuable.
