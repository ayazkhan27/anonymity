Phones Belong Out of the Driver's Hands

Most people now carry a cell phone everywhere, and the habit of checking it has followed them into the driver's seat. That has led to an argument over whether drivers should be allowed to use phones at all while a car is moving. I think drivers should not handle or interact with a phone in any meaningful way while driving. The only exceptions should be emergency calls and hands-free navigation that is set up before the trip starts.

The first reason is attention. Driving looks routine, but it never stops asking things of the driver. You have to judge the speed of oncoming traffic, keep track of a cyclist at the edge of the road, and notice the child who might run out after a ball. Reading a text takes the driver's eyes off the road for several seconds. At highway speed, a car covers the length of a football field in that time. Nobody would agree to drive that distance blindfolded, yet a quick glance at a screen has the same effect.

Some people say hands-free calling solves the problem. It helps, but it does not fix it. Studies of distracted driving have found that a conversation pulls the mind away from the road even when both hands stay on the wheel. Talking to someone in the passenger seat is different, because a passenger can see the road too and will usually stop talking when traffic gets heavy. The person on the other end of a call cannot see anything, so the conversation keeps going while the driver's attention slips. That is why I would limit even hands-free use to short, necessary calls.

The other side has a fair point about navigation and emergencies. Phones have mostly replaced paper maps, and a driver following spoken directions is safer than one squinting at street signs or unfolding a map on the steering wheel. In an emergency, such as a crash ahead or a medical problem, a phone call can save lives. A sensible rule would allow these uses, provided the route is entered before the car moves and the phone sits in a mount instead of in the driver's hand.

Everything else can wait. Texts, social media, music playlists, and email do not need an answer in the next five minutes, and no message is worth a collision. Strong laws, steady enforcement, and phone settings that silence notifications while driving would all help. In the end, though, it comes down to each driver deciding that the road deserves their full attention.
