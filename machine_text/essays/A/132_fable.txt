Why One Opinion Is Rarely Enough

When my cousin was deciding whether to take a job in another city, she asked her father first. He told her to stay, because the pay was only a little higher and she would be far from family. If she had stopped there, she probably would have turned the offer down. Instead she kept asking, and what she learned from three other people changed her mind. Her experience shows why talking to more than one person usually leads to a better choice: each person sees only part of the picture, each person has blind spots of their own, and hearing several views helps you figure out what you actually want.

The first reason is simply that no single person knows everything. My cousin's father knew about money and family, but he had never worked in her field. A former coworker who had made a similar move told her that the new company was known for training people well, and that two years there would open doors the old job never would. A friend who lived in that city told her what rent really cost and which neighborhoods were worth the commute. None of these people had the whole answer. Put together, though, their answers covered the salary, the career, and the daily life, which is far more than any one of them could have given her.

The second reason is that advice always comes through the adviser's own feelings. Her father loved her and did not want her to leave, so his advice leaned toward staying, even though he believed he was being practical. This is not dishonesty; it is just how people are. A doctor who performs surgery may be quicker to recommend surgery, which is why patients facing a serious operation are often told to get a second opinion. When you ask only one person, you cannot tell which part of the advice is about your situation and which part is about them. When you ask several, the personal slant of each one tends to show, because the others do not share it.

The third reason is less obvious. Listening to different opinions teaches you about your own. When my cousin's friend described the city, she noticed she was excited. When her father listed the reasons to stay, she noticed she was looking for arguments against him. Her reactions told her something that no adviser could have told her directly. Disagreement among advisers is useful for the same reason. If everyone says the same thing, you can feel fairly confident. If they split, you know exactly where the hard question is and can spend your thinking there.

Of course, asking more people does not mean counting votes, and at some point more opinions only add noise. The decision still belongs to the person who has to live with it. My cousin took the job, and she made that choice herself. But she made it knowing far more than she did after the first conversation, and that is the real value of asking around.
