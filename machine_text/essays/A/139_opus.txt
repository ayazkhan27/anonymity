When people face a hard decision, they often turn to someone they trust for advice. But one person, however wise, sees the problem from only one angle. Asking several people usually leads to a better choice, because each person brings different experience, different blind spots, and different ways of thinking about the same question.

The first reason is that people know different things. Imagine a high school senior deciding which college to attend. Her parents might focus on cost and how far the school is from home. A teacher might know which programs are strong in the subject she wants to study. An older friend who already goes to one of the schools could describe what the dorms, the classes, and the social life are actually like. No single one of these people has the full picture, but together they cover most of what she needs to know. Each conversation fills in a gap the others left open.

Second, multiple opinions help a person notice bias. Everyone's advice is shaped by their own life. A man who lost money starting a restaurant may warn everyone against opening a business, while a friend who succeeded may make it sound easy. If someone only listened to one of them, he would get a distorted view. By hearing both, he can see that the outcome depends on things like planning, location, and luck, and he can make a more balanced judgment about his own situation.

Third, when several people agree, that agreement carries weight, and when they disagree, the disagreement is useful too. If three different doctors recommend the same treatment, a patient can feel more confident that it is the right one. If the doctors give different answers, that tells the patient the decision is not simple, and he should ask more questions before committing. Either way, he learns something he would have missed by talking to only one doctor.

Finally, explaining a problem to more than one person helps the person asking. Each time he describes his situation, he has to put his thoughts in order. Often he discovers what he really wants just by hearing himself talk it through, or by noticing which advice makes him feel relieved and which makes him uneasy.

Of course, collecting opinions does not mean letting others decide. The final choice still belongs to the person who has to live with it. But a decision made after hearing several voices is usually better informed, more balanced, and more confident than one made after hearing just one.
