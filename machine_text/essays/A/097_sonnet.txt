I agree with the principal's decision that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. School is about more than grades, and these activities teach lessons that a classroom rarely can.

First, extracurriculars help students build skills they will need as adults. A student who works on the yearbook learns to meet deadlines, write captions, and cooperate with classmates who have different strengths. Someone on the student council learns how to run a meeting, listen to complaints, and turn an idea into a real event like a food drive. These are the same abilities employers look for, and they are hard to practice in a lecture.

Second, these activities help students make friends and feel they belong. Many teenagers, especially new ones, eat lunch alone and go home right after the bell. When my cousin joined the soccer team in ninth grade, he went from knowing almost no one to having a group of teammates who encouraged him to keep his grades up. Belonging to a team or club gives students a reason to enjoy coming to school.

Some people argue that a requirement is unfair to students who already have jobs or family duties. That concern is fair, but the rule only asks for one activity, and the school can offer flexible choices, such as a club that meets once a week. A quiet student who loves chess or art can find a place just as easily as an athlete.

Because the benefits are so strong, I support the requirement. It gives every student a chance to grow, connect, and discover something new about themselves.
