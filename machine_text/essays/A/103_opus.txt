Phones and the Road

Almost everyone carries a cell phone now, and most of us feel lost without one. Still, the place where a phone does the most harm is behind the wheel. Drivers should not be allowed to hold or handle a phone at all while the car is moving. The one exception should be hands-free tools such as voice navigation, set up before the trip begins.

The main reason is attention. Driving takes your eyes, your hands, and your mind at the same time. A text message pulls away all three. Glancing down for five seconds at highway speed means traveling the length of a football field without looking at the road. In that stretch a child can step off a curb or the car ahead can brake hard. Nobody is a good enough driver to react to something they never saw.

People often say they can handle it, that they only check a message at red lights or on empty roads. The trouble is that habits do not stay in safe places. Someone who reads texts at a stoplight soon reads them while rolling forward, and then while merging. A clear rule that says the phone stays down is easier to follow than a loose rule full of exceptions, and it is easier for police to enforce.

Some argue that phones also make driving safer. Maps keep drivers from getting lost and making sudden turns, and a phone lets someone call for help after a crash. These are fair points, which is why the rule should allow mounted, voice-controlled navigation and emergency calls. What it should forbid is typing, scrolling, watching videos, and holding the phone to talk.

A phone call or a message can almost always wait ten minutes. A life cannot be brought back. If a message truly matters, a driver can pull over, park, and answer it safely. Keeping phones out of drivers' hands is a small sacrifice that protects the driver, the passengers, and everyone else sharing the road.
