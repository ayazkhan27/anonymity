Every June, my cousin used to come home with the same packet: forty pages of math review and a reading log for a novel he had not picked. By August the packet was still in his backpack, and he filled it in over two frantic nights before school started. He learned nothing from it except how fast he could write. I think about that packet whenever people argue over summer projects, because it shows what a teacher-designed assignment often turns into. Summer projects should be designed by students, with a teacher approving the plan.

The main reason is that students work harder on something they chose. During the school year a teacher can keep a class on task with deadlines, reminders, and grades every week. In July there is nobody checking. The only thing that gets a fifteen-year-old to open a notebook on a hot afternoon is some interest in what is inside it. A student who likes cars might spend three weeks figuring out why an old lawn mower engine will not start, and along the way read manuals, measure parts, and write up what went wrong. That is reading, math, and writing, and no one had to nag him.

Designing the project is also part of the learning. Deciding on a question, estimating how long it will take, and finding out halfway through that the plan was too big are skills that worksheets never teach. A girl in my neighborhood decided to interview her grandmother about leaving Vietnam and turn it into a short book. She had to cut it to four chapters when she ran out of time, and she said that was the hardest decision of the whole summer.

Teachers worry, fairly, that some students will choose something lazy, like watching a television series and calling it research. That is why approval matters. A one-page proposal turned in before the last day of school lets the teacher reject weak ideas and push good ones further. The teacher still sets the standard; the student supplies the subject.

A summer project only works if it gets done with some thought. Students are far more likely to do that with a project that is theirs.
