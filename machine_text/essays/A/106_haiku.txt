Drivers should not be allowed to use cell phones in any capacity while operating a vehicle. A car demands constant attention, and a phone competes for that attention no matter how it is used. Holding a device with one hand and typing or swiping with the other is an obvious problem, but the more serious danger lies in the mind. Research on distracted driving has repeatedly shown that conversations, even hands-free ones, pull attention away from the road. A driver on a call may fail to notice a brake light ahead or a pedestrian stepping off a curb, and these lapses often last only a few seconds, long enough to cover the distance of a football field at highway speed.

Some argue that hands-free devices solve the problem. They reduce the physical distraction, but they do not remove the mental one. The brain cannot fully focus on two demanding tasks at once, so a driver listening to a message or composing a reply is operating with less capacity for the road. A strict rule with no exceptions is easier to follow and to enforce than a patchwork of allowed behaviors that drivers must judge for themselves in the moment.

Emergency calls are the strongest objection, and they can be handled without permitting general use. A driver can pull over safely to call for help, and passengers can place calls or manage navigation on the driver's behalf. Those options protect the public without asking every driver to make split-second decisions about whether a conversation is safe.

Cell phones have made life more connected and convenient, but a moving car is not the place for that connection. Banning phone use entirely while driving protects drivers, passengers, and everyone sharing the road, and it asks very little in return.
