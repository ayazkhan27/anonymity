I agree with the principal's decision that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. Some people will say it is unfair to make students do something outside of class, but I think the benefits are too important to leave to chance. Activities teach skills that classrooms cannot, they help students make friends, and they give students a reason to care about school.

First, extracurricular activities teach lessons that are hard to learn from a textbook. When a student joins the soccer team, she learns how to show up on time, how to keep going when she is tired, and how to lose a game without giving up. A student who works on the yearbook learns how to meet deadlines, interview people, and fix mistakes before the pages go to print. Student council members learn how to speak in front of a crowd and how to plan an event with a small budget. These are the same skills that employers and colleges look for. A test score shows what you know, but an activity shows what you can do with other people.

Second, activities help students find friends and feel like they belong. A big school can feel lonely, especially for a new student or a shy one. In a classroom, you mostly sit and listen, so it is hard to get to know anyone. In the drama club or on the robotics team, you spend hours working toward the same goal, and friendships grow naturally. My cousin moved to a new town in eighth grade and did not know anybody. She only joined the school choir because she had to pick something, but by the end of the year the choir was where all her closest friends came from. If the requirement had not existed, she might have spent that year eating lunch alone.

Third, being part of an activity can make students try harder in their classes. Many teams require players to keep their grades up to stay eligible, which gives athletes a clear reason to study. Even without that rule, students who feel connected to their school are more likely to come every day and pay attention. An activity gives a student something to look forward to after the last bell, and that good feeling often carries over into the rest of the day.

Some people argue that students are already busy with homework, jobs, or family duties. That is a fair concern, but the rule only asks for one activity, and there are many choices. A student with little free time could join a club that meets once a week, like the chess club or the school newspaper. The point is not to overload anyone. It is to make sure no one misses out.

For these reasons, I support the principal's decision. One activity is a small requirement that can give students skills, friendships, and a stronger connection to their school.
