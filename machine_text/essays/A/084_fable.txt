One Activity Is Not Too Much to Ask

When I first heard that our principal wants every student to join at least one extracurricular activity, my reaction was to groan. I already have homework, chores, and a little brother who needs watching on Thursdays. But after thinking about it for a while, I changed my mind. I agree with the decision, as long as the school keeps the rule flexible.

The strongest reason is that students often do not know what they like until someone pushes them to try it. Last year my friend Marcus signed up for the yearbook only because his mother insisted. He complained for two weeks. By October he was staying late to fix photo layouts, and now he talks about studying graphic design. He would never have found that out by going home at 3:00 and playing video games. A requirement gives quiet or unsure students the nudge they would not give themselves.

Activities also teach things that classes do not. In math, if I make a mistake, only my grade suffers. On the soccer team, if I skip practice, ten other people pay for it. Student council members have to listen to complaints about the lunch menu, argue politely, and then actually plan something with a budget. Those are skills adults use every day, and a worksheet cannot teach them.

There is a social reason too. Our school has about nine hundred students, and it is easy to spend three years knowing only the people in your own classes. A club mixes grades and friend groups. A sixth grader in the chess club ends up sitting across from an eighth grader, and suddenly the hallway feels less frightening. Students who feel they belong somewhere tend to show up more and cause less trouble.

I know the objections. Some students have jobs, long bus rides, or family duties, and others are simply shy. These are fair points, and they are why the rule needs room in it. The school should offer clubs that meet during lunch or advisory period, so nobody has to stay after the buses leave. It should count activities with small time commitments, like helping in the library once a week. And a student who truly cannot manage any of it should be able to talk to a counselor and get an exception.

With those adjustments, one activity is a small demand. It costs an hour or two a week, and in return a student might gain a skill, a friend, or an interest that lasts for years. Most of us will not volunteer for that on our own. The principal is right to ask.
