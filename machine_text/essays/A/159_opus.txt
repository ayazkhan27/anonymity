Every June, students walk out of school carrying the same packet: a reading log, a set of math worksheets, maybe a book report with a rubric stapled to the front. By August, most of those packets have been finished in a frantic weekend or not at all. If the goal of a summer project is to keep students learning, then schools should let students design their own projects, with teachers acting as guides rather than authors.

The strongest reason is motivation. A teacher-designed project asks every student to care about the same thing at the same time, and that rarely happens. A student who loves baseball might groan at a generic essay assignment but spend hours calculating batting averages, tracking pitch speeds, and comparing players across decades. That is real statistics, and he would do it willingly. When students choose the subject, the work stops feeling like a chore that follows them home and starts feeling like something they own. Learning that comes from curiosity tends to stick far longer than learning done to satisfy a checklist.

Second, designing a project is itself a skill worth practicing. In school, nearly every decision is made for students: what to read, when it is due, how long it should be. Adults, however, constantly have to set their own goals and plan how to reach them. A summer project gives students a low-stakes chance to practice this. A girl who decides to build a small vegetable garden has to research which plants grow in her climate, budget for seeds and soil, keep a schedule for watering, and figure out what went wrong when the tomatoes split. No worksheet teaches that kind of planning and problem-solving.

Third, student-designed projects make use of the resources students actually have. Summers look very different from family to family. One student spends July at a grandparent's farm, another works part-time at a restaurant, and another babysits younger siblings every day. A single teacher-designed assignment cannot fit all of these lives. A student-designed project can. The restaurant worker might study how the kitchen manages food costs; the babysitter might write and illustrate a picture book for the children she watches.

Critics argue that without teacher design, students will choose projects that are too easy or abandon them entirely. This concern is fair, but it is an argument for structure, not for teachers making every choice. Schools can require students to submit a short proposal before summer begins, explaining what they will do, what they hope to learn, and how they will show it. Teachers can approve, suggest changes, and check in once or twice by email. At the end, students can present their work to the class, which builds accountability and lets everyone learn from each other's summers.

Summer is one of the few times students have the freedom to follow their own interests. Schools should take advantage of that freedom instead of filling it with another packet. When students design their own projects, they learn the material, and they also learn how to teach themselves.
