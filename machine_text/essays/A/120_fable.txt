When my cousin Dana was deciding whether to leave her nursing job for a travel contract in Arizona, she asked five people what they thought. At first I figured she was just stalling. But watching how it played out changed my mind about why people ask around before a big decision.

The first reason is simple: no single person knows everything. Dana's mother told her to stay because the hospital had good benefits. That was true, but her mother had never worked a travel contract. A coworker who had done two of them explained things nobody else could, like how housing stipends are taxed and how hard it is to make friends when you move every thirteen weeks. Each person saw one piece of the picture, and Dana needed all of the pieces.

Second, every adviser has a bias, and hearing several of them lets you notice it. Her mother wanted her close to home. Her recruiter earned a commission if she signed. Her best friend missed her already and said so. None of them were lying, but each had a reason to lean one way. If Dana had talked only to the recruiter, she would have heard nothing but sunshine. With five opinions side by side, she could tell which points came from facts and which came from what the speaker wanted.

Third, when people who disagree about most things agree on one point, that point is probably solid. Almost everyone told Dana to read the cancellation clause carefully. She did, and found the agency could end the contract with one week of notice. She negotiated for two.

Asking several people does not mean letting them vote. Dana still made the choice herself, and she went against her mother's advice. What the conversations gave her was a list of questions she would not have thought to ask and a clearer sense of what she was risking. She took the contract, kept her old license active just in case, and came back eight months later with savings and no regrets.

One opinion is a guess. Several, compared honestly, start to look like a map.
