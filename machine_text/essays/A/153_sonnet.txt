Summer break is a long stretch of unstructured time, and schools that assign summer projects are right to worry about what students lose when they stop practicing. But the question of who should design those projects matters a great deal. I believe summer projects should be student-designed, with teachers acting as guides who set a few clear guardrails. When students choose their own work, they put in more effort, they learn skills no worksheet can teach, and they are far more likely to finish.

The strongest argument for student-designed projects is motivation. A teacher-designed packet arrives in June as one more obligation, and most students treat it that way. They leave it in a backpack until late August and then rush through it, copying answers or guessing. A project a student invented is different, because the student already cares about the outcome. A girl who loves baking might test how altitude, humidity, and oven temperature change the rise of bread, recording results in a notebook all summer. A boy fascinated by video games might learn basic programming and build a small game of his own. Neither would need to be reminded to work. The effort comes from curiosity, and curiosity is the most reliable engine of learning there is.

Student-designed projects also teach things that teacher-designed ones rarely do. Planning a project means deciding what question to ask, what steps to take, how much time each step needs, and how to tell whether the work succeeded. Those are exactly the skills students will need in college and in jobs, where no one hands over a detailed assignment sheet. A student who sets a goal in June and has to manage her own deadlines through the heat of July is practicing self-discipline in a way that a teacher's checklist cannot replicate. If a plan falls apart halfway through, she must decide how to fix it. That experience of troubleshooting is worth more than a perfectly completed worksheet.

Critics of this idea raise a fair concern. Some students, left alone, will choose something too easy or will never start at all. Teachers have the training to know what a rigorous project looks like, and a standard assignment guarantees a baseline of learning for everyone. These worries are real, but they argue for guided student design, not for teacher design. A school can require each student to submit a short proposal in the spring, and the teacher can approve it, suggest changes, or push for more ambition. The teacher might ask for a mid-summer check-in by email and a final presentation in the fall. This keeps the structure that struggling students need while still leaving the choice of topic in their hands. A student who proposes something trivial can be sent back to improve it, just as a student who proposes something wonderful can be encouraged.

There is also a fairness argument. A single teacher-designed assignment assumes that every student has the same resources and the same interests. In reality, one student may have a library nearby and a parent at home, while another works a summer job and shares a single computer with siblings. A required project that involves museum visits or expensive supplies punishes the second student. When students design their own projects, they can build on what is available to them. A student who spends the summer helping in a family restaurant could write a business plan for it, interview customers, and chart which dishes sell best. That is real learning drawn from real life, and it costs nothing extra.

Finally, student-designed projects make the fall classroom better. When students return with something they built, wrote, or discovered, they have something to share, and their classmates learn from it. Presentations of such projects spark conversation in a way that comparing completed packets never could. Teachers also learn what their students care about, which helps them teach more effectively all year.

Summer should keep students learning, but learning that feels forced rarely lasts. By letting students design their own projects under a teacher's guidance, schools turn a dreaded assignment into a chance for ownership, growth, and genuine curiosity. That is the kind of summer work students will remember, and the kind that will actually prepare them for what comes next.
