I agree with my principal's decision that every student should take part in at least one extracurricular activity. A school is more than a place where students memorize facts for tests. It is also where they learn how to work with others, manage their time, and discover what they care about. Requiring one activity guarantees that every student gets those chances, including the ones who would never choose to take them on their own.

Consider a student who spends every afternoon at home playing video games and avoids anything that requires talking to strangers. Without a requirement, that student may never try anything new. With a requirement, he might join the yearbook staff and discover that he enjoys photography. He learns to meet deadlines, to take direction from an editor, and to see a project through to a printed page. Those skills will matter far more in a job interview than a perfect score on a history quiz.

Sports offer a similar benefit. A student who joins the junior varsity soccer team learns that effort matters even when she is not the best player on the field. She learns to show up for practice in the rain, to accept criticism from a coach, and to rely on teammates who depend on her. These lessons about persistence and responsibility are hard to teach in a classroom, but they grow naturally through sustained participation in a team.

Student council is another strong option. Students who serve on it hear the concerns of their classmates and must find practical ways to address them. One council in my own school spent a semester arguing about the cafeteria menu, then spent the next semester organizing a food drive that collected more than two thousand cans for a local shelter. Those students learned that a problem can be broken into steps, and that leadership often means doing unglamorous work that nobody applauds.

Critics argue that forcing students into activities takes away their freedom and adds pressure to already busy schedules. This concern deserves a fair hearing. However, the requirement says "at least one," not "as many as possible," and the list of options is broad enough to suit almost any interest, from chess club to the robotics team to the community garden. A student who already works part-time or cares for younger siblings can still find an activity that fits, and many clubs meet only once a week. The requirement asks for commitment, not for a schedule that overwhelms.

Ultimately, the decision rests on a simple belief: every student deserves a chance to belong to something larger than their own classes. Students who participate in extracurricular activities graduate with stronger friendships, clearer goals, and more confidence in their own abilities. Those benefits should not depend on luck or on whether a student happens to have a parent who signs them up. By making participation a shared expectation, the principal ensures that every student has access to the experiences that help them grow. For these reasons, I strongly support this decision.
