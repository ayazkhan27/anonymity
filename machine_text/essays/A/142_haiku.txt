Summer projects should be student-designed. Teachers already shape the school year, and a summer assignment handed down from above often becomes a task to finish rather than a reason to learn. When students choose their own projects, they invest real curiosity in them.

Consider a student who loves baseball but dreads her required reading list. If she designs a project comparing batting statistics across decades, she must learn statistics, research history, and write clearly to explain her findings. The same student, assigned a generic book report, may skim the book and forget it by September.

Student design does not mean no guidance. Teachers can set requirements, such as a minimum time commitment, a deliverable, and a short proposal for approval. This structure keeps projects rigorous while letting students own their learning.

Ownership also builds the skill that matters most after school: deciding what to learn and how to pursue it. A summer project designed by a teacher teaches compliance; one designed by a student teaches independence.
