Who Should Design the Summer Project?

Every June, students in many schools carry home a packet along with their report cards. Inside is the summer project, the assignment meant to keep their minds working through ten weeks of vacation. Schools that require these projects have a reasonable goal, since research on "summer slide" shows that students can lose a month or more of reading and math progress between June and September. The harder question is who should decide what the project looks like. I believe summer projects should be designed by students, with teachers setting a few clear requirements and approving the plan before school lets out.

The strongest reason is motivation. During the school year, a teacher can remind, encourage, and check in. In July, nobody is standing over a student's shoulder. The only thing that gets a project done well, instead of thrown together the night before school starts, is the student's own interest in it. A teacher-designed project is the same for everyone, so it fits almost no one exactly. I remember being assigned a packet of thirty reading-response worksheets one summer. I finished them in two days at the end of August, copying the format from one page to the next, and I could not have told you a week later what any of the books were about. Compare that with a friend of mine who was allowed to choose her own topic for a science class. She spent the summer testing which homemade fertilizer grew the tallest tomato plants in her backyard, measured them every three days, and came back in September with a notebook full of data and a real opinion about compost. Nobody had to nag her, because the project was hers.

Student-designed projects also fit the reality of summer better. Students do not all spend their break in the same way. One works thirty hours a week at a grocery store. Another lives with grandparents in a different state, and a third has to watch younger siblings every day. A single assignment written in May cannot account for all of that. A student who designs the project can build it around the life they will actually be living. The student with the grocery job could track how prices change over the summer and explain why. The one visiting grandparents could record interviews about what the town was like fifty years ago and write a family history. These projects demand reading, writing, math, and research, and they are possible precisely because they grow out of circumstances a teacher could never have predicted.

A third benefit is that designing a project is itself a skill worth learning. Choosing a question, deciding how to answer it, estimating how long each step will take, and adjusting when something goes wrong are exactly the abilities that colleges and employers say young people lack. A teacher-designed project does all of that thinking for the student and leaves only the labor. When a student has to plan the work, the planning becomes part of the education.

The obvious objection is that students, left alone, will pick something easy. Some would. A student might propose watching a television series and writing a paragraph about it. Another concern is that teachers know what skills the next grade requires and students do not. Both concerns are fair, but they are arguments for guidance and not for taking the design away from students entirely. The teacher can require that every project include a certain amount of reading, a written product of a set length, and some connection to the coming year's subjects. Students would submit a one-page proposal in May, and the teacher would send weak ones back for revision. That way the teacher protects the quality and the student keeps the ownership.

Summer projects exist because schools want learning to continue when the building is closed. Learning that continues without supervision has to be driven by the learner. A project a student has chosen, planned, and shaped around their own summer is far more likely to be finished with care and remembered afterward than a packet handed out to the whole class. Schools should set the standards and then let students design the work.
