Phones Belong in the Glovebox

Most people carry a cell phone everywhere, and many of us feel uneasy when it is out of reach. That habit follows us into the car. It is tempting to think a quick glance at a text or a short call is harmless, but driving is the one daily activity where a few seconds of distraction can end a life. For that reason, drivers should not use cell phones while operating a vehicle, with only narrow exceptions for emergencies and hands-free navigation set up before the trip begins.

The main problem is attention. Driving asks the brain to track speed, distance, other cars, pedestrians, signals, and road conditions all at once. Reading a text pulls a driver's eyes off the road for several seconds. At highway speed, a car covers the length of a football field in about five seconds. A driver who looks down for that long is driving blind. A child stepping off a curb or a car braking suddenly does not wait for someone to finish a sentence.

Some people argue that hands-free calls solve the problem. Keeping both hands on the wheel helps, but it does not fix the real issue, which is mental distraction. Studies of driver behavior have shown that people deep in a phone conversation react more slowly and miss things right in front of them, even when they are looking forward. Talking to a passenger is different, because a passenger can see the road and will usually stop talking when traffic gets tense. The person on the other end of a phone call has no idea what the driver is facing.

Others say the rule would be impossible to enforce, or that adults should be trusted to judge their own limits. But we already limit freedom on the road when one person's choices put others at risk. Drunk driving laws and speed limits exist for the same reason. Most drivers who text believe they are the exception, and that confidence is exactly what makes the habit dangerous.

There are reasonable exceptions. Calling 911 to report a crash or a fire should always be allowed, and a map program running on a mounted phone, set before the car moves, is no more distracting than a dashboard screen. Beyond that, the phone should stay silent and out of hand. Nothing on it is worth a life, whether the driver's or a stranger's.
