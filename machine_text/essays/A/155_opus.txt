Summer break gives students something the school year rarely does: time that belongs to them. When schools require summer projects, they should let students design those projects, within a few clear guidelines from teachers. A project a student chooses is more likely to be finished, more likely to be remembered, and more likely to teach the habits that matter after graduation.

The first reason is motivation. A teacher-designed packet of worksheets usually gets finished in a panic the night before school starts, if it gets finished at all. A project the student picked works differently. A girl who loves baking might keep a journal of recipes she scales up and down, practicing fractions and ratios without anyone forcing her. A boy obsessed with baseball could track his favorite team's statistics all summer and write a report on which players were most valuable. Because they care about the subject, they keep at it for weeks instead of rushing for a few hours.

Second, student-designed projects build skills that a ready-made assignment cannot. When a teacher hands out a project, the hardest decisions are already made: what to study, how to measure progress, and what the finished product should look like. Those are exactly the decisions adults face at work and in life. A student planning a summer project has to choose a question, set a schedule, find sources, and fix problems when the plan falls apart. Learning to manage a project alone may be worth more than any single fact a worksheet could cover.

Third, summer is a chance to reach beyond the classroom. Students live in different places and have different opportunities. One might spend July on a relative's farm, another might volunteer at a library, and another might be caring for younger siblings. A single teacher-designed assignment ignores all of that. A student-designed project can turn those real experiences into learning, such as a study of soil quality on the farm or an interview series with library patrons.

Critics worry that students left alone will choose projects that are too easy. That concern is fair, but the answer is structure, not control. Teachers can require a short proposal in June, set minimum expectations for research and writing, and ask for a presentation in the fall. With that frame in place, students still own the work, and they return in September with something they are proud to share.
