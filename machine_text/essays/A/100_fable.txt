Phones and the Driver's Seat

Almost everyone I know carries a phone, and almost everyone I know has looked at it while driving. That includes me. The habit is so ordinary that it hardly feels like a risk, which is exactly the problem. I believe drivers should not be allowed to hold or handle a phone while a vehicle is moving, but I do not think the law should ban every possible use. A phone mounted on the dashboard giving directions is a different thing from a phone in the hand, and the rules should treat them differently.

The case against handheld use is simple. Driving takes eyes, hands, and attention, and a phone demands all three. Reading a text takes a few seconds, and at highway speed a car covers the length of a football field in that time. The driver is effectively blindfolded for that stretch. Nothing has to go wrong in the car itself; a child stepping off a curb or a truck braking ahead is enough. Safety agencies attribute thousands of deaths every year to distracted driving, and many of those victims were not the ones holding the phone. That last point matters most to me. A person who refuses to wear a seat belt mostly gambles with his own life. A person who texts at forty miles an hour gambles with the lives of strangers who never agreed to the bet.

Some people argue that they are skilled enough to manage both. I doubt it. People are poor judges of their own attention, and the drivers who feel most confident are often the ones drifting between lanes. The only way to find out you were wrong is to crash.

Still, a total ban would go too far. Navigation apps have replaced paper maps, and a voice telling me to turn left in half a mile is safer than unfolding a map on the steering wheel. A phone is also how a driver calls for help after a breakdown or reports an accident. Hands-free calls are not harmless, since a conversation still pulls the mind away from the road, but they keep the eyes up and the hands on the wheel, and they are hard to tell apart from talking with a passenger. A law that forbids all of this would be widely ignored, and a law that people ignore teaches them to ignore the next one.

So the line should be drawn at touch. Set the route before leaving the driveway, let the phone speak, and keep it out of reach until the car is parked. A message can wait ten minutes. The person in the crosswalk cannot.
