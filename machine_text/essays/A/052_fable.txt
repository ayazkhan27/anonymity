Learning From Home Should Be a Choice

When my cousin broke her leg in the fall of her sophomore year, she missed almost five weeks of school. Her teachers sent packets home, but a packet cannot explain why you got a chemistry problem wrong. By the time she came back on crutches she was so far behind in two classes that she spent the rest of the semester trying to catch up. If her school had let her join class by video, she would have lost a few days instead of a month. That is the main reason I think students would benefit from being able to attend classes from home. I do not think it should replace school, but it should be available.

The clearest benefit is for students who cannot physically get to the building. Injuries and long illnesses are the obvious cases, but there are others. Some students live an hour or more from their school and spend two hours a day on a bus. Some have to stay home with a younger sibling when a parent works a shift that cannot be moved. In bad winters, whole districts close for a week at a time. In every one of these situations the student is able to learn and wants to learn, and the only thing in the way is the trip. Video classes remove that obstacle. The student hears the same lesson, asks questions as they come up, and turns in the same work as everyone else.

A second benefit is that home can be a better place to concentrate for certain students. A classroom with thirty people in it is loud, and a lot of time goes to things that are not learning: waiting for the room to settle, changing classes, dealing with whoever is acting up that day. A student who is shy or anxious may never raise a hand in that room but will type a question into a chat box without thinking twice. I have a friend who took an online math course over the summer and said it was the first time he ever asked a teacher to go back and repeat a step. Recorded lessons help too, because a student who did not understand something the first time can watch it again instead of pretending to follow along.

Distance learning also teaches a skill that regular school mostly does not, which is managing your own time. Nobody rings a bell at home. A student has to log in on their own, keep track of deadlines, and decide when to take a break. This is hard, and some students will fail at it at first. But college courses and many jobs now work exactly this way, and it is better to learn it at sixteen with a teacher checking in than at nineteen with nobody watching.

The strongest argument against all this is that students at home miss out on other people. That is true. You do not make friends through a screen the way you do at lunch, and a lab or a band rehearsal does not work over video. Some students would also simply stop paying attention with a phone and a bed a few feet away. These are real problems, and they are why I would not want anyone forced into it. But they are reasons to keep distance learning as an option and to let families and teachers decide who it suits. A student who starts slipping can be brought back into the building.

Schools already accept that students learn in different ways. Letting some of them learn from a different place, when they have a good reason to, follows from the same idea. My cousin would have kept her grades, and nobody else would have lost anything.
