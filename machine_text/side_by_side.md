# The dial, side by side

Four essays from the dial experiment, each in four versions: the student's essay (PERSUADE 2.0, CC BY 4.0), the machine draft written to the same assignment and length, the draft after two plain revisions ("make it read as a student of that grade wrote it"), and the draft after two rounds with the dial (the same instruction plus a sheet of target values). The target for each draft was the measured profile of a different student essay on another assignment, so the student essay shown here is the length match, not the target.

The essays were picked by rule from the 48 with both arms complete: the median, the largest and the smallest improvement in gap to the target, and one pair the blind judge scored for the dial. Nothing was picked by eye.

### Machine draft

When I had to choose between two summer programs last year, my first instinct was to ask my mom and go with whatever she said. She thought the robotics camp was a safe bet because it looked good for college. But then I talked to my older cousin, who had done a similar program, and he told me it was mostly lectures. A friend from my class told me the art workshop had a small group where everyone actually got to make things. By the end of the week I had three different opinions, and I made a much better decision than I would have made with only one.

The first reason multiple opinions help is that every person sees only part of the picture. My mom knew what colleges like, but she had never attended either program. My cousin had real experience, but he was a different kind of student than I am. Each person had useful information the others lacked, and putting those pieces together gave me something close to the whole story.

A second reason is that people have biases they often cannot see. A parent may want what feels safest, a friend may want you to join the same activity they chose, and a salesperson wants you to buy. When several people disagree, you notice those leanings. If three people who do not know each other all warn you about the same problem, that warning is probably worth taking seriously. If only one person says it, you can weigh it more carefully.

Finally, hearing other views forces you to think about your own reasons. When my cousin said the robotics camp was mostly lectures, I had to ask myself what I really wanted from the summer. I realized I wanted to build things with my hands, not just sit and listen. Without that conversation, I might never have figured that out.

Of course, more opinions are not always better. Too many voices can be confusing, and in the end the choice belongs to the person who has to live with it. But asking a few trusted people, especially people with different experiences, gives you more information, exposes hidden bias, and sharpens your own thinking. That is why I would always rather ask several people than rely on one, even when the first answer sounds convincing.

### Machine draft

Drivers Should Put the Phone Down

Almost everyone carries a cell phone now, and it is easy to feel that the device belongs in every part of life, including the driver's seat. But a car is a two-ton machine moving at speeds that leave very little room for error. Drivers should not be allowed to use cell phones in any capacity while operating a vehicle, because even a brief lapse in attention can cost a life, and no message or call is worth that risk.

The strongest argument against phone use is how the human brain works. Driving demands constant attention: watching the road, checking mirrors, judging the speed of other cars, and anticipating what a pedestrian or cyclist might do. Researchers have long found that people are poor at doing two demanding mental tasks at once. What feels like multitasking is really rapid switching, and each switch leaves a gap during which the driver is effectively blind to what is happening ahead. At highway speed, the few seconds it takes to read a text cover the length of a football field. A lot can go wrong in that distance.

Some people argue that hands-free calling is a safe compromise. The idea is appealing, since both hands stay on the wheel. Yet the danger of a phone call is not only in the hands. It is in the mind. Studies of drivers using hands-free systems show reaction times that are slower and a tendency to miss things in plain view, such as a stopped car or a changing light. A conversation pulls attention away from the road even when the eyes are pointed forward. If a law allows hands-free use, it sends a false message that the practice is safe, and drivers will feel free to talk for long stretches.

Another objection is that a complete ban is unrealistic, or that people need their phones for navigation and emergencies. These concerns are fair, but they have simple answers. Navigation can be set up before the trip begins, and a driver who gets lost can pull over to check a map. In a real emergency, such as an accident or a medical crisis, a driver can stop in a safe place and call for help. The rule would be about use while the vehicle is being operated, so a parked car on the shoulder is a different matter. Nearly every legitimate need can be met by waiting a few minutes.

A clear rule is also easier to understand and enforce than a complicated one. When laws permit some uses but forbid others, police officers cannot easily tell whether a driver was dialing a number, reading a text, or checking a map. Drivers can claim they were doing the allowed thing. A blanket rule removes that confusion. It also changes habits. Seat belt laws and drunk driving campaigns show that a firm standard, backed by public education, can change what people think is normal. Not long ago, many people shrugged at driving without a seat belt. Today most would never consider it.

Finally, the issue is about more than the driver. A distracted person puts passengers, other motorists, children walking to school, and workers on the roadside in danger. Those people did not choose the risk. Using a phone behind the wheel is a decision made by one person that can be paid for by someone else.

Cell phones are useful tools, but the road is not the place for them. Drivers should wait until the car is safely stopped, because every message can be answered later, and a life lost cannot.

### Machine draft

Dear Principal,

I am writing to urge you to adopt Policy 1, which allows students to bring phones to school and use them during lunch and other free times, provided they are switched off during class. A total ban may seem like the simpler choice, but it would cost students more than it protects.

First, phones are part of how students stay safe. Many families rely on them to confirm pickup times, check in after a bus is late, or reach a parent during an emergency. Under Policy 2, those students would have no reliable way to reach home during the school day.

Second, a ban treats students as if they cannot manage a simple rule. Policy 1 already sets a clear boundary: phones stay off during class. Students who respect that boundary keep their phones; those who break it can lose the privilege. This teaches responsibility, which students will need long after they leave school.

Third, free time matters. Lunch and passing periods are when students eat, talk with friends, and decompress. A phone is often how they read the news, reply to a family message, or simply take a break from the crowd. Removing that option does not make those minutes more productive. It only makes them more crowded.

Finally, enforcing a full ban would demand constant monitoring, lockers, and confiscations, all of which take staff time away from students. Policy 1 is easier to supervise because the rule is simple and visible.

I respectfully ask you to choose Policy 1. It keeps students connected, builds responsibility, and gives them reasonable freedom within clear limits.

Respectfully,
A Concerned Student

Why More Than One Opinion Leads to Better Choices

Last spring my cousin Dana had to decide between two colleges. One was a large state university close to home with a generous scholarship. Each opinion changed the shape of the decision. Dana's story shows why asking more than one person usually leads to a better choice than asking only one.

The first reason is that every person sees a problem from a different position. Dana's parents were thinking about debt, because they had lived through years of paying off loans. Her friend was thinking about happiness, because she knew how often Dana talked about the ocean. Neither was wrong, but each was looking at only part of the picture. When someone asks only one person, they get one slice of the situation and may mistake it for the whole. When they ask several, the slices begin to overlap, and the person can see which concerns matter most and which are simply one individual's worry.

The second reason is that different people know different facts. Advice is not only a matter of taste. It is often a matter of information. Dana's teacher knew about the coastal lab partnership because he had sent two former students there. Her parents had no idea it existed, and her friend had never looked into it. If Dana had stopped after the first conversation, she would have made her choice without a piece of knowledge that changed everything. The same thing happens when people buy a used car. A friend who loves driving may talk about how smooth it feels, while a mechanic notices rust under the frame. Neither can replace the other, and a buyer who hears both is far less likely to be surprised later.

When several people disagree, the tilts tend to cancel each other out, and what remains is more reliable. Doctors understand this, which is why patients facing a serious diagnosis are often encouraged to get a second opinion. A second doctor has no reason to protect the first one's conclusion, and if both agree, the patient can proceed with real confidence.

When she listened to her friend, she felt relieved. Those reactions were data about what she wanted. Disagreement forced her to explain her reasons out loud, and in doing so she sorted out her own priorities. A single adviser can easily become a voice that simply gets obeyed or ignored. A group of advisers makes the person do the actual work of weighing.
