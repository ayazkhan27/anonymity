Last spring my cousin Dana had to pick between two things. She could take a job in Denver or stay home and finish her nursing degree. She asked her mom first, and her mom said right away, "Stay. Your family is here." If Dana had stopped asking right there, she probably would have stayed. But she kept asking people, and I think that is a big reason it turned out good for her. Asking more than one person for advice helps you make a better choice because you find out what you were missing, you hear from people who know different things, and you have to figure out why you really want something.

The first reason is that everybody who gives advice has a bias, and when you ask a few people the biases kind of cancel out. Dana's mom wanted her to stay because she would miss her. That does not mean her advice was bad, but it was partly about her mom's feelings. Then Dana asked her friend Marcus, who moved away for work a long time ago. He told her leaving was the best thing he ever did. His advice came from his own life too. Neither of them was lying, they were just telling how it went for them. When Dana heard both she could see where each answer was coming from. If you only hear one opinion it sounds like a fact. When two people disagree you can tell they are just opinions.

The second reason is that different people know different stuff. Dana also talked to one of her nursing teachers, and the teacher brought up something her mom and Marcus never thought about. The job in Denver was in billing and not taking care of patients. If she quit school without her degree, it would be really hard to go back to nursing later. Dana was so excited about the money that she never thought about where the job would take her in five years. Her teacher had seen a lot of students do the same thing and knew how it usually went. Dana could not have got that from people who loved her but did not work in that field. One person, even a nice one, only knows a small piece of what you need to know.

The third reason is that when you ask a bunch of people, you have to explain your problem over and over, and explaining it makes you think. By the third time Dana talked about it, she noticed she kept saying the same thing. She did not really want to leave her program. She just wanted to feel like she was getting somewhere. Every person asked her a little different question, and each one made her say what mattered most to her. Sometimes the best part of someone's opinion is not their answer, it is the question they make you think about. Marcus asked her, "What would you regret more?" and she realized she would regret quitting her degree way more than saying no to a job.

Of course, asking people has limits. If you ask too many people it can get confusing, and someone who asks ten people might just be looking for one person to say what they already want to hear. It is not about counting votes. Dana did not just go with whatever most people said. She thought about the reasons behind what each person said. I think a good rule is to ask a few people who come from different places, and then look at why they think what they think instead of just what they picked.

In the end Dana stayed and finished her degree, and later she got a better job at a hospital in Denver. Her mom was right about waiting, Marcus was right about moving, and her teacher was right about the timing. Nobody had the whole picture, but all together they gave her enough of it to feel sure about her choice. That is why asking more than one person does not mean you have bad judgment. It makes your judgment stronger, because a decision based on a few honest opinions is a lot safer than one based on only one person.
