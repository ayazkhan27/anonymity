Last spring my cousin Danielle had to decide if she should take a job at a hospital two states away or stay at the clinic where she had worked for four years. She asked her mom first, and her mom told her to stay. If Danielle had stopped there, she would have had an answer, but it would have been based on one person. She went on to ask three more people, and what she picked in the end made more sense because of it. I think her story shows why you should ask more than one person before you make a big choice.

The first reason is that every person only sees part of the situation. Danielle's mom thought about how far away the new city was and how Danielle would not know anybody there. Her supervisor at the clinic told her there was no room to move up where she was, and that the hospital would train her in things the clinic did not have. A friend who had moved for work a few years before talked about money. She said Danielle would have to pay a deposit on an apartment, more for car insurance, and plane tickets to come home for holidays. All of these people were telling the truth. Each one just knew about a different piece, and Danielle needed every piece to see the whole thing.

The second reason is that people mix what they want into what they tell you, and most of the time they do not even notice. Danielle's mom did think staying made more sense, but she also did not want her daughter living six hours away. If you only hear one opinion, you can not tell which part is facts and which part is what the person wants. When you hear from a few people, you can see where one of them is different from the rest. Danielle noticed that her mom was the only one who kept talking about her being alone. That let her think about it as one thing on the list and not the whole list.

The third reason is that when you compare what people say, you find out what the real question is. If everybody says the same thing, you can pretty much count on it. All four people Danielle asked said the hospital would do more for her career, so she did not have to keep thinking about that part. The part they did not agree on was if moving was worth leaving her family and paying all that money. That showed her what she still had to figure out by herself. It is kind of like when somebody goes to a second doctor. If both doctors say to get the same surgery, the person can go ahead with it. If the doctors say different things, the person knows to ask more questions first.

Some people say that asking a lot of people just mixes you up. I get that, because a person could keep asking forever so they never have to decide. But that only means you should ask a few people who know about the subject, not that you should ask one. Also, getting advice is not the same as doing what you are told. Danielle did not count votes. She listened to everybody and then she picked for herself, so the choice was really hers.

In the end Danielle took the hospital job. She also asked to start a little later so she could save up money for the move, and that idea came from her friend. She has been there eight months now, and she just started the training her supervisor told her about. She says she is glad she went. One talk with one person would not have gotten her to that plan. It took four, and that is why I think people should ask more than one person for advice.
