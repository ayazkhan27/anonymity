When my cousin Dana was deciding whether to leave her nursing job for a travel contract in Arizona, she asked five people what they thought. At first I thought she was just stalling. But watching it play out showed me why asking around is a smart and helpful thing to do before a big choice.

The first reason is easy. One person can only know some things. Dana's mom told her to stay because the hospital had good benefits. That was true, but a coworker who had done two travel contracts gave her great advice that her mom could only guess at. She explained how housing money is taxed and how to make friends when you move every thirteen weeks. Each person had one helpful piece of the picture, and Dana was glad to get them all.

Second, every person who gives advice wants something, and hearing a lot of them helps you see it. Her mom loved having her close to home. Her recruiter earned a nice bonus if she signed. Her best friend loved hanging out with her and said so. All of them were honest, but each one had a reason to lean one way. The recruiter alone would have told her only the sunny, fun parts. With five opinions side by side, Dana could tell which points were true facts and which were wishes.

Third, when people who like different things agree on one point, that point is probably strong and worth trusting. Almost everyone told Dana to read the cancel part of the contract with care. She did, and she found the agency could end the job with one week of notice. She asked for two and happily got it.

Asking a lot of people is different from letting them vote. Dana still made the choice herself. The talks gave her a list of good questions and a clearer idea of what she was risking. She took the contract, kept her old license active to be safe, and came back eight months later with savings and a proud smile.

One opinion is a guess. But a few good opinions, compared in an honest way, are like a helpful map, and I think that is the best way to choose.
