More Than One Voice

Last spring my cousin Dana had to decide if she should take a job in another city. She asked her mom first, and her mom told her to stay close to home. If Dana had stopped there, she would have said no to the job. Instead she kept asking. She talked to four different people, and she changed her mind twice before she picked an answer she trusted. Watching her do that showed me why asking a few people for advice is a better and smarter way to make a good choice than asking just one.

The first reason is that every person only sees part of the picture. Dana's mom thought about how lonely a new city can feel, because she moved once herself and hated it. Dana's old manager thought about the pay and the title, because that is what he knows. A friend who already lived in that city talked about rent, traffic, and how long it took her to meet people. Each of them was right about one piece. When Dana put all their answers side by side, she had something like a whole map. One person gives you one angle. A few people show you the corners you could not see from where you were standing.

The second reason is that people want things for themselves too. Dana's mom wanted her nearby, and that is a sweet, kind, loving thing for a mom to want. It is just a different thing from what helps Dana's career. Here is another example. A salesman at the store tells you which phone to buy. He might be honest, and he also gets paid when you buy it. If you only ask him, you have no way to tell where his advice stops and his paycheck starts. So you also ask a friend who owns the phone, and you read what a repair guy says about it. When three people who want different things all say the same thing, you can feel safe and sure trusting it.

Third, it helps when people disagree. Two people Dana respected gave her opposite answers, so she had to figure out why. Her manager thought she wanted to move up fast. Her friend thought she cared most about free time. That showed Dana a question she had skipped. What did she want out of the next five years? Doctors know this already. People who need surgery are told to get a second opinion. If the second doctor agrees, the patient goes in calm and confident. If the second doctor says something else, the patient learns there is more to think about, and that is good to know before anyone picks up a scalpel.

Of course, you can ask too many people. Ten opinions can leave you more mixed up than two, and at some point you are just putting off the choice. The answer is to listen and then think, instead of counting votes. Dana took the job, and it was her own choice. The talks showed her what mattered to her and what could go wrong, and then she chose for herself.

Asking a lot of people still leaves the choice in your own hands. It gives you better stuff to work with. You get more facts, you get a fair check on what one person wants, and you get smart questions you would have missed. I think that is the best gift good advice can give, and it makes me feel hopeful and brave about the big choices I will have to make someday. Dana has lived in her new city for eight months now. She loves her job, she has made great friends, and she is proud and happy she listened to more than one voice. She still calls her mom every Sunday, and they laugh the whole time.
