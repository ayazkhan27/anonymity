Last spring my cousin Dana had to decide about a job offer in Denver. She could take it, or she could stay in her hometown and finish her nursing degree. She asked her mother first. Her mother said right away, "Stay. Family is here." If Dana had quit asking there, she would have stayed. Instead she kept asking, and that is why her choice turned out so good. I think asking more than one person helps you make a great decision, because it shows you blind spots, gives you different kinds of knowledge, and helps you understand your own reasons.

The first good thing is that every adviser has a bias. Dana's mother loves her and wanted her close, which is a sweet reason. It was partly about the mother's feelings, though. Then Dana asked her friend Marcus, who moved away for work years ago. He said leaving was the best thing he ever did, and he was really happy about it. His view came from his own life too. Both of them were honest and kind. Each one just told the story they lived. When Dana heard both, she could see where the advice came from. One opinion looks like a fact. Two opinions that disagree look like what they are, which is a point of view.

The second benefit is that different people know different things. Dana also talked to her nursing instructor, who is a wonderful teacher and a smart person. The instructor saw something nobody else saw. The Denver job was in billing instead of patient care, and leaving school early would make it hard to come back to the work Dana loves. Dana was so excited about the nice salary that she forgot to think about five years later. Her instructor had watched many students make the same jump and knew how it ended. Her mother and Marcus love Dana, but they do not work in nursing, so they could not give her that fact. One adviser, even a very caring one, only holds one piece of the puzzle.

Third, talking to several people makes you explain your problem again and again, and explaining is a great way to think. By the third talk, Dana heard herself say the same thing every time. She wanted to finish her program. What she really wanted was to feel like she was going somewhere. Each person asked a different question, and each question helped her find what mattered most. Sometimes the best part of advice is the question it gives you. When Marcus asked, "What would you regret more?" she knew right away. She would regret quitting her degree far more than turning down a job.

Of course, there is a limit. Too many voices can be confusing, and someone who asks ten people might only be hunting for a person to say what she wants to hear. You should not just count votes. Dana did not follow the crowd. She looked at the reasons behind each view. A smart rule is to ask a few people with different jobs and different lives, then look at their reasons.

In the end, Dana stayed, finished her degree, and got an amazing job at a hospital in Denver. She was so proud and happy! Her mother was right about waiting, Marcus was right about moving, and her instructor was right about timing. Together they gave her a wonderful, full picture, and she chose with joy and confidence. That is why asking more people is a smart move, and it is a strength to be proud of. A choice built on many honest voices is a lovely, strong choice, and I am glad Dana had so many good people to love and trust.
