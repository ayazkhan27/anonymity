More Than One Voice

Last spring my cousin Dana had to decide if she should take a job in a different city. The first person she asked was her mom, and her mom said to stay close to home. If Dana had stopped there she would of said no to the job. But she didn't stop, she kept asking people. She talked to four people total and changed her mind two times before she finally picked. I was around for most of it and it made me think that asking a few people for advice is better than asking only one.

My first reason is that each person only knows part of what is going on. Dana's mom kept talking about how lonely it is in a new city. She moved one time when she was younger and hated it, so thats what she thinks about. Dana's old manager talked about the money and the job title because that is the stuff he knows. Then Dana had a friend who already lived in that city, and she told her about the rent and the traffic and how long it took her to make friends there. Nobody was wrong. But nobody knew everything either. When Dana put all the answers together it was kind of like a map that was almost finished. One person can only give you one piece of the map. When you ask a bunch of people you get the pieces you couldn't see by yourself.

My second reason is that people want things for themselves, even people who love you. Dana's mom wanted her to live nearby. That is a nice thing to want, but it is not the same as what is best for Dana's job. It's like when you go to buy a phone and the guy at the store tells you which one to get. He might be telling the truth, but he also gets paid more if you buy it. If he is the only one you ask, you can't tell which part is real advice and which part is him wanting money. But if you also ask a friend who has that phone, and you look up what somebody who fixes phones says, then it kind of evens out. If three people who all want different things tell you the same answer, you can trust it a lot more than just one of them.

My third reason is that it actually helps when people disagree. This one sounds weird but it's true. Two people Dana really respected told her opposite things, so she had to figure out why. It turned out her manager thought she wanted to move up fast at work, and her friend thought she cared more about having free time. That made Dana ask herself something she never asked before, which was what does she even want for the next five years. Doctors do this too. When somebody needs surgery, a lot of times they get told to go get a second opinion from another doctor. If the second doctor agrees, the person feels better about it. If the second doctor doesn't agree, then the person knows it's not as simple as it sounded, and I would want to know that before they cut me open.

Some people might say that asking too many people just makes you confused. That is true sometimes. Ten opinions could be worse than two, and some people keep asking just so they don't have to decide. But you are not supposed to count votes like an election. Dana didn't take the job because three out of four people said to. She took it because all that talking showed her what she cared about and what could go wrong. Then she picked for herself.

In conclusion, asking more than one person doesn't mean other people decide for you. It gives you more facts and better questions to think about, and it keeps one person from pushing you their way. Dana has lived in her new city for eight months now and she likes it. She still calls her mom every Sunday.
