Why Asking More Than One Person Is Better

Last spring my cousin Dana had to pick between two colleges. One was a big state university close to home, and it gave her a good scholarship. The other was a small private school three states away with a better marine biology program. Dana has loved marine biology since she was ten. She asked her parents first, and they told her to take the scholarship. Then she asked her best friend, who said she should follow the marine biology. Last she asked her old science teacher, and he said something nobody else thought of. The state university had a summer program at a coastal lab, so maybe she could get both. Every person she asked changed how she saw the choice. Dana's story shows that asking more than one person usually gets you a better decision than asking just one.

The first reason is that everybody sees a problem from a different side. Dana's parents were worried about debt because they spent years paying off their own loans. Her friend was thinking about Dana being happy, since she heard Dana talk about the ocean all the time. Neither of them was wrong, but they were each only seeing part of it. If you ask just one person, you get one piece and you might think it is the whole thing. When you ask a few people, the pieces start to overlap, and you can tell which worries are really important and which are just one person's fear.

The second reason is that different people know different things. Dana's teacher knew about the coastal lab because he had sent two of his old students there. Her parents had no idea it even existed, and her friend never looked it up. If Dana had stopped after the first person, she would have made her choice without knowing something that changed everything. It is the same when someone buys a used car. A friend who likes driving might say how smooth it feels, but a mechanic could see that there is rust under the frame. One of them can't replace the other, and a buyer who hears both is a lot less likely to get a bad surprise later.

Another good thing about more opinions is that they protect you from bias. Everybody who gives advice wants something, even if it is small. Dana's parents wanted her to stay closer to home, partly because they would miss her. These feelings are normal and not really dishonest, but they push the advice in one direction. When a bunch of people disagree, the pushes kind of cancel out, and what is left is easier to trust. Doctors know this, which is why people with a serious diagnosis are told to get a second opinion. The second doctor has no reason to protect what the first doctor said, so if they agree, the patient can feel sure.

Also, hearing lots of views helps a person figure out what they think. When Dana listened to her parents, she noticed she felt defensive, and that told her something. When she listened to her friend, she felt relieved. Those feelings showed her what she really wanted. Because people disagreed, she had to explain her reasons out loud, and that helped her sort out what mattered most. With only one adviser, it is easy to just obey them or ignore them. With a group, you have to do the real work of thinking it through.

This does not mean more is always better. If you ask twenty people, you could end up with a huge mess of answers that confuses you instead of helping. It is smart to pick people who know about the subject or who know you well. In the end, the choice belongs to the person who has to live with it. But a small group of different voices, maybe three or four, gives you facts you did not have, points of view you missed, and a way to check for anyone's hidden bias.

Dana ended up going to the state university, and she joined the coastal lab the next summer. She says she would have chosen differently, and probably worse, if she only listened to the first person who answered. Asking around did not make the choice for her. It made the choice an informed one, and that is what good advice is supposed to do.
