More Than One Voice

Last spring my cousin Dana had to decide if she should take a job in another city. She asked her mom first, and her mom told her to stay close to home. If Dana had stopped there, she would have said no to the job. Instead she kept asking. She talked to four different people, and she changed her mind twice before she picked an answer. Watching her do that showed me why asking a few people for advice is a better way to make a choice than asking just one.

The first reason is that every person only sees part of the picture. Dana's mom thought about how lonely a new city can feel, because she moved once herself and hated it. Dana's old manager thought about the pay and the title, because that is what he knows. A friend who already lived in that city talked about rent, traffic, and how long it took her to meet people. Each of them was right about one piece. When Dana put all their answers side by side, she had something like a whole map. One person gives you one angle. A few people show you the corners you could not see from where you were standing.

The second reason is that people want things for themselves too. Dana's mom wanted her nearby. That is not a bad thing for a mom to want, but it is a different thing from what helps Dana's career. Here is another example. A salesman at the store tells you which phone to buy. He might be honest, but he also gets paid when you buy it. If you only ask him, you have no way to tell where his advice stops and his paycheck starts. So you also ask a friend who owns the phone, and you read what a repair guy says about it. When three people who want different things all say the same thing, you can trust it more.

Third, it helps when people disagree. Two people Dana respected gave her opposite answers, so she had to figure out why. Her manager thought she wanted to move up fast. Her friend thought she cared most about free time. That showed Dana a question she had skipped. What did she want out of the next five years? Doctors know this already. People who need surgery are told to get a second opinion. If the second doctor agrees, the patient goes in calm. If the second doctor says something else, the patient learns there is more to think about, and it is better to know that before anyone picks up a scalpel.

Of course, you can ask too many people. Ten opinions can leave you more mixed up than two, and at some point you are just putting off the choice. The answer is to listen and then think, instead of counting votes. Dana took the job, and it was her own choice. The talks showed her what mattered to her and what could go wrong, and then she chose for herself.

Asking a lot of people still leaves the choice in your own hands. It just gives you more to work with. You get more facts, you get a check on what one person wants, and you get questions you would have missed. It does not make the choice easy, and it does not promise that things will turn out right. Dana has lived in her new city for eight months now. She likes her job, but the first winter was hard and she still gets lonely on weekends. She says she would have been angry at herself if she had only listened to one person, because then she would never have known what she was giving up. She still calls her mom every Sunday. When I have to make a big choice someday, I am going to ask more than one person before I decide.
