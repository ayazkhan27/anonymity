When my cousin Dana was deciding whether to leave her nursing job for a travel contract in Arizona, she asked five people what they thought. At first I thought she was just stalling. Then I watched how it went, and it showed me why asking around is a smart thing to do before a big choice.

The first reason is simple. One person can only know some things. Dana's mom told her to stay because the hospital had good benefits. That was true. A coworker who had done two travel contracts knew things her mom could only guess at. She explained how housing money is taxed and what it is like to move every thirteen weeks. Each person had one piece of the picture, and Dana needed all of them.

Second, every person who gives advice wants something, and hearing a lot of them lets you see it. Her mom wanted her close to home. Her recruiter got a bonus if she signed. Her best friend loved hanging out with her and said so. None of them lied, but each one had a reason to lean one way. The recruiter alone would have told her only the sunny, fun, exciting parts and left out the rest. With five opinions side by side, Dana could tell which points were facts and which were wishes.

Third, when people who want different things all say the same point, that point is probably strong. Almost everyone told Dana to read the cancel part of the contract slowly. She did, and she found the agency could end the job with one week of notice. That was not fair to her. She asked for two weeks and got it.

Asking a lot of people is different from letting them vote. Dana still made the choice herself. The talks gave her a list of questions to ask and a clearer idea of what she was risking. She took the contract and kept her old license active just in case. She came back eight months later with savings, a proud smile, and great stories.

One opinion is only a guess. A few opinions, when you compare them side by side, work more like a map. I think that is the best and safest way to make a big choice, and I am glad Dana showed me.
