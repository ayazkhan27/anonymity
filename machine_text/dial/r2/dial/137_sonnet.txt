Why More Than One Opinion Is Better

Last spring my cousin Dana had to choose between two colleges. One was a state university close to home. It offered her a scholarship. The other was a small private school three states away. It had a marine biology program, and she has been interested in the ocean since she was ten. She asked her parents and they told her to take the money. She asked her friend Mia and Mia said to follow the ocean. Then she asked her old science teacher. He said something nobody else had thought of. The state university runs a summer program at a coastal lab, so she could possibly get both. Every opinion changed the choice. Dana's story shows why asking more than one person is better than asking only one.

The first reason is that every person looks at a problem from a different spot. Dana's parents worried about debt because they had trouble paying off loans when they were young. Mia thought about happiness because she had heard Dana talk about the ocean for years. Nobody was wrong. Each one only saw a small part of the choice. If you ask just one person you get one piece, and you might think it is the whole thing. When you ask a few people the pieces start to fit together. You can tell which worries are large and which ones belong to one person.

The second reason is that people know different facts. Advice is not only opinion. It is also information. Dana's teacher knew about the lab because he had sent two former students there. Her parents did not know the program existed, and Mia never looked it up. If Dana had stopped after the first talk she would have missed it. It is like buying a used car. A friend who likes driving says the car feels smooth. A mechanic looks under it and finds rust on the frame. A buyer who hears both is less likely to get a surprise later, and that can save a lot of money.

The third reason is that many opinions work against bias. Everybody who gives advice wants something, even if it is small. Dana's parents wanted her to stay close because they would miss her. Mia was leaving town too, so she liked the idea of Dana taking a chance like she did. That is natural and it is not mean, but it pushes the advice in one direction. When people disagree, the pushes cancel out a little and the answer gets more balanced. Doctors follow this idea. A person with a serious diagnosis is often told to get a second opinion. The second doctor does not have to defend the first one. If they agree, the patient has more reason to go ahead with the plan.

Hearing different views also helps you understand yourself. When Dana listened to her parents she felt defensive, and that told her something. When she heard her friend she felt relieved. Those reactions showed her what she wanted. The disagreement made her explain her reasons out loud, and she sorted out what mattered most to her. One adviser is easy to obey or to ignore. A group makes you do the work of thinking, and that work takes time.

I have seen this in my own life too. Last year I could not decide between band and the soccer team. I was stressed out and my stomach hurt for a week. My mom said band. My coach said soccer. My older brother asked which one I would miss more if I quit. That question helped me most, and I would not have heard it if I had only talked to one person. In the end I joined both.

None of this means more is always better. If you ask twenty people you get a loud pile of answers and it gets confusing. It makes sense to pick people who know the topic or know you well. The choice still belongs to you, since you are the one who lives with it. Three or four voices can give you facts you were missing, views you did not see, and some protection against bias.

Dana went to the state university and joined the coastal lab the next summer. She says she is glad she asked around, since she would have picked differently if she had listened to only the first person. Asking did not make the choice for her. It gave her more to work with. She still misses her old town some days and the lab hours are long, and she says the choice was a trade. I think anyone facing a choice like hers can use the same method. It costs a few conversations, and it gives you more of the picture.
