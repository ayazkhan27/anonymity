When I had to choose between two summer programs last year, my first idea was to ask my mom and do whatever she said. She thought the robotics camp was a safe choice because it would look great for college. Then I talked to my older cousin, and he said it was not fun at all, just long lectures. A friend from my class told me the art workshop was great, with a small group where everyone got to make cool things. By the end of the week I had three opinions, and I made a better choice than I would have with only one.

The first reason multiple opinions help is that every person sees only part of the picture. My mom knew what colleges like, and she felt sure about it, yet she had never gone to either program. My cousin had real experience, and he is a different kind of student than I am. Each person had good information the others did not have, and putting it together gave me a clear, helpful look at the whole story.

A second reason is that people have bias they often cannot see. A parent may want what feels safe, a friend may want you to join the same thing they picked, and a salesperson wants your money, which is not nice. When several people disagree, you can notice those leanings. If three people who do not know each other all warn you about the same problem, that is a strong sign and worth taking seriously.

Finally, hearing other views helps you think about your own reasons. When my cousin said the robotics camp was dull, I asked myself what I truly wanted from the summer. I wanted to build things with my hands and share ideas with happy people, not sit and listen all day.

Of course, more opinions are not always better. Too many voices can be confusing, and in the end the choice belongs to the person who has to live with it. I feel glad I did not ask just one person. Kids who ask a few trusted people get a fair chance to choose, and that makes for a fun, wonderful summer.
