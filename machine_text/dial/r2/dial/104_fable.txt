Hands on the Wheel, Phone in the Glovebox

Almost everyone I know carries a phone, and almost everyone I know has looked at it while driving. That includes me. My phone is where my friends are, where my music is, and where my mom texts me to ask what time I will be home. Still, I believe drivers should keep their phones put away while the car is moving. The only exceptions should be an emergency call and a map that is set up before the trip starts. I think this rule is a good and fair idea, and I think most people would be happy with it once they got used to it.

Driving asks for three things at the same time. It asks for eyes on the road, hands on the wheel, and a mind that is paying attention. Texting takes all three. In drivers ed, my teacher told us that a car on the highway goes about the length of a football field in the five seconds it takes to read a message. For that whole distance the driver is basically looking at a screen, and that is not safe. A kid could step off the curb, or the car in front could stop, or the light could turn red. A driver who is looking up has a great chance to react and keep everybody safe.

Some people say hands-free is the answer. The phone sits on the dashboard and the driver talks to it. That is better than texting, but the best and safest choice is still to let the call wait. My teacher said that a driver who is deep in a phone conversation can look straight at something and still miss it, because the brain is busy with the call. A friend riding in the passenger seat is different. A friend can see the traffic and will kindly get quiet when the freeway gets crowded. The person on the other end of the call has no idea what the road looks like.

There is also the question of what is fair. When I drive, I share the road with people walking, people on bikes, and whole families in other cars. They trust me to watch out for them. Nobody asked them if I could read a text at forty miles an hour. I love my friends and I enjoy hearing from them, but I love the idea of everyone getting home safe even more, and a text is worth much less than that. Caring about strangers is a good and kind thing to do, and putting the phone in the glovebox is an easy way to show it.

The rule can have some room in it. A driver who sees someone who needs help should be able to call 911. Map apps are helpful too. A friendly voice that says "turn left in half a mile" lets the driver keep looking ahead. The key is to type in the address while the car is parked. If the route changes, the driver can pull over. The same goes for music. Pick a favorite playlist before shifting into drive.

Some people say that police can't see into every car. That is true, but a clear law still does a lot of good. My grandpa told me that people used to skip seat belts all the time. Then the law changed, and now buckling up feels normal. A phone law could work the same way. It would tell new drivers like me that the message can wait.

Every call and text will still be there when the car is parked. So will the videos and the group chat. A crash is different, because nobody can take it back, and I would not feel good about a text that hurt somebody. What matters most is that people get home. Putting the phone away is a small thing to do, and it is not hard. It gives us a calm ride and more days with our friends and family.
