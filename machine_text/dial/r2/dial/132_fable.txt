Why One Opinion Is Not Enough

Last year my cousin had to decide if she should take a job in another city. The first person she asked was her dad. He told her to stay, because the pay was only a little higher and she would be far from the family. If she had stopped there, she probably would have said no to the job. Instead she kept asking, and what she heard from three other people changed her mind. I think her story shows why you should talk to more than one person before you make a big choice. Each person only knows part of the picture, each person has their own feelings mixed in, and hearing a few different views shows you what you actually want.

The first reason is that no one knows everything. My cousin's dad knew about money and family, but he had never worked in her kind of job. A person she used to work with had made the same kind of move, and she said the new company trains people well. She said two years there would open doors the old job never would. Then a friend who lives in that city told her what rent costs and which neighborhoods are close to the office. None of them had the whole answer. When you put all three together, she knew about the pay, the career, and what every day would be like. One person could not have told her all of that.

The second reason is that advice comes with the feelings of the person giving it. Her dad did not want her to move away, so of course he said to stay. He was not lying. He thought he was just being practical. That is how people are. It is the same with doctors. A doctor who does surgery all day might suggest surgery faster, and that is why people get a second opinion before an operation. If you ask only one person, you can't tell which part of the advice is about you and which part is about them. If you ask a few people, you can see it, because the others do not feel the same way.

The third reason is one I did not think of at first. Listening to other people teaches you about yourself. When my cousin's friend talked about the city, she noticed she was really excited. When her dad listed reasons to stay, she noticed she kept looking for ways to answer him. Nobody could have told her that. She had to hear both sides to find out. It also helps when people do not agree. If everyone says the same thing, you can feel pretty sure. If they say different things, you know which question you need to think about the most.

Asking more people does not mean you count votes. The choice still belongs to the person who has to live with it. My cousin took the job. She misses her family sometimes, but she is happy there now. She chose it herself, and she knew a lot more than she did after the first talk. That is why I believe you should ask around before a big decision. It takes more time, and it gave my cousin a better choice in the end.
